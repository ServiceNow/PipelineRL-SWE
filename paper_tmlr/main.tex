\begin{abstract}
    Routers typically choose a model per query by trading predicted correctness against cost, but most price each model at a fixed cost, as if every query drew the same number of output tokens. The output length of reasoning models varies by an order of magnitude across queries. We ask when per-query cost is worth predicting, what a prediction captures, and how cheaply it can be had. We predict \textbf{both} success probability \textbf{and} each route's output length from the prefill activations of a single small frozen 4B LLM, a scout that reads the prompt and generates nothing, with one linear probe per route. On 6,500 MMLU-Pro, 1,000 Omni-MATH and 341 LiveCodeBench test tasks, predicted costs reduce spending at matched accuracy by 23.4\%, 31.5\% and 27.8\% relative to median-length pricing: two fifths to over two thirds of what perfect per-query cost knowledge would save (40--58\%), and more than every alternative estimator we evaluated, including a text-embedding model of the prefill's size. On a mixture of all nine benchmarks, one probe saves 22\% beyond a router that knows each query's benchmark, and 10\% beyond one that also prices from difficulty within it. Across nine datasets the saving follows two conditions: output length must vary within a route more than prices differ between routes, which holds for reasoning models but much less for non-reasoning models on the same problems, and some estimator must be able to predict that variation from the prompt. We bound the second from above without any estimator, by how much of a route's length the problem fixes, and from below by the best estimator we tried; where the two are far apart, no estimator we tried captures the saving. Pricing from difficulty alone matches the probes where length tracks difficulty, but the dedicated probe is what generalises: it keeps most of its saving with 20 labelled problems, when a new model is added from 10 examples, and on four of seven benchmarks it was not trained on, without target labels; text-embedding and item-response estimators fall behind it in all three settings. Every small prefill we tried (0.6--4B, four model families) works, and fully fine-tuning a small model on the same labels does not match a linear probe of a frozen one.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Routing reasoning models requires predicting both correctness and cost before generation. A model's price per token is known in advance, but the number of tokens it will write for a given problem is not. A model that is cheap on average can be expensive on a particular problem: on LiveCodeBench, a single model's output length differs 11--121$\times$ between its 10th- and 90th-percentile problems (\cref{fig:spread}).

\centering

\label{fig:spread}
\end{figure}

Prefill routers predict correctness from a small encoder's prompt activations; the prefill router of Varshney et al.~\citep{prefill} explicitly prices outputs at median training length. We read output length from the same activations of a small frozen \emph{scout} model, which only reads the prompt and generates nothing (\cref{fig:overview}). Cost prediction adds one linear probe per route to an existing prefill router, without another encoder pass.

This raises three questions, which organise the paper:
\begin{itemize}
\item When is per-query cost worth predicting?
\item What does a prediction of it capture?
\item How cheaply can a router obtain it: with how many labels, for how many new models, and on how many new benchmarks?
\end{itemize}

We contribute:
\begin{itemize}
\item a measurement of what per-query cost knowledge is worth on nine datasets at billed prices: 29--61\% of spending at matched accuracy wherever reasoning lengths vary, and much less with non-reasoning routes on the same problems, where no estimator we tried captures much of it (\cref{sec:headroom,sec:when});
\item prefill cost probes that save 23.4--31.5\% relative to median pricing on three large test sets and beat mean-length, prompt-feature, text-embedding and ZeroRouter cost estimators (\cref{tab:fresh});
\item a mechanism: per-query length is mostly a property of the problem, shared across routes; where it tracks difficulty, pricing from the success probes suffices, and where it tracks the work a question requires, only a dedicated probe captures it. Each failure we find has an identifiable cause: too little headroom, or headroom that no estimator we tried reads from the prompt (\cref{sec:mechanism,sec:when});
\item generalisation: the dedicated probe keeps most of its saving with few labels, for a new model onboarded from a handful of examples, and on four of seven benchmarks it was not trained on; the external estimators do not, and pricing from difficulty transfers only between benchmarks where difficulty means the same kind of work (\cref{sec:general});
\item mixed traffic: on a mixture of all nine benchmarks, the cost probe saves 22.4\% beyond pricing by benchmark identity, the signal that explains much of what quality routers gain~\citep{llmrouterbench,tasktype}, and 9.9\% beyond benchmark and difficulty together (\cref{sec:general});
\item controls: the gain holds for every small prefill we tried (0.6--4B, four families); a text-embedding model of the same size reads cost worse under every probe; fully fine-tuning a small model on the same labels does not match a linear probe of the frozen prefill; and our probes on fixed layers match the prefill router's full success pipeline on four of five sets (\cref{sec:ablations}).
\end{itemize}
Our router does not beat the most accurate single route, deepseek-v4-flash, at that route's own accuracy. Its saving lies below it: giving up five points of accuracy saves 15--40\% relative to deepseek-v4-flash alone on six of the nine datasets, and under a hard per-query budget the router beats deepseek-v4-flash alone only at the tightest cap we tested (\cref{sec:deploy}).

\paragraph{Related work.}
Learned routers pick one model per query from the prompt, trading predicted quality against price: RouteLLM learns a strong-versus-weak decision from preference data~\citep{routellm}, RouterBench standardizes the evaluation~\citep{routerbench}, and IRT-Router models query difficulty and model ability with item response theory~\citep{irtrouter}. Closest to us, the prefill router of Varshney et al.\ predicts each model's correctness from a small encoder's prompt activations~\citep{prefill}, and pre-generation activations are known to encode whether a model will fail~\citep{encodefailures}. These routers, like SWE-Router for agentic software engineering~\citep{swerouter}, price each model at a per-model constant; the prefill router uses the median training output length, which is our main reference. Fewer routers predict per-query cost: MixLLM regresses output length on query embeddings~\citep{mixllm}, CARROT uses embedding nearest neighbours or a fine-tuned RoBERTa~\citep{carrot}, GraphRouter predicts cost on a query--model graph~\citep{graphrouter}, Route-To-Reason predicts reasoning-model length from text embeddings~\citep{routetoreason}, ZeroRouter prices queries by item-response difficulty bins~\citep{zerorouter}, and SCOPE estimates a query's cost from retrieved past behaviour~\citep{scope}. R2-Router routes over pairs of a model and an output-length budget, so length is controlled rather than predicted~\citep{r2router}, and SCX Router reads task type, difficulty and an expected output length from a small decoder's KV cache as properties of the task, not of each candidate model~\citep{scx}. We compare against the embedding-based and ZeroRouter estimators directly, with every estimator given the same success predictions. Recent large-scale evaluations find that routers land close to an oracle that assigns each dataset its best model, and attribute much of their gain to coarse domain structure~\citep{llmrouterbench}; a static assignment by task type closes most of the gap to the per-query oracle on a small benchmark~\citep{tasktype}. Both concern which model answers correctly; we measure what per-query cost is worth beyond the benchmark a query comes from.

Cascades instead call models in sequence and stop when an answer is accepted: FrugalGPT learns a scorer that decides whether to escalate~\citep{frugalgpt}, AutoMix uses self-verification~\citep{automix}, and Dekoninck et al.\ unify routing and cascading~\citep{cascaderouting}. A cascade pays for the cheap attempt on every query; we route once, before generation. Without a verifier, a pre-generation router beats the best cascade on four of five datasets, LiveCodeBench among them~\citep{escalation}. Activation-based length prediction also supports serving and scheduling of a single model~\citep{alps,egtp,outlets}; we predict the length of other models from one shared prefill. Hosted open-weight models vary across providers in accuracy, length and price~\citep{samemodel,gateways,modelequality}, and FACET selects among providers of a fixed model per task from measured outcomes~\citep{facet}.

\section{Cost Prediction and Routing}
For query $x$ and route $m$ (a model at a fixed reasoning effort, served by an endpoint), let $p_m(x)$ be the probability of a correct answer and $c_m(x)$ the expected generation cost. One route is chosen before generation,
\begin{equation}
m^*(x;V)=\arg\max_m\{V\hat p_m(x)-\hat c_m(x)\},
\label{eq:route}
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the value of a correct answer; sweeping $V$ trades accuracy for cost. Every query is answered once, with no verifier or fallback.

\paragraph{Probes.}
We concatenate mean- and last-token activations from eight layers of a frozen Qwen3-4B~\citep{qwen3} and fit one linear probe~\citep{alain2016probes} per route and target: for success an L2-regularized logistic regression, calibrated by Platt scaling~\citep{platt1999}; for cost a ridge regression of log mean output length, corrected for the bias of exponentiating by Duan's smearing~\citep{duan1983}. The predicted length is priced at the route's token rates, $\hat c_m(x)=r_m^{\rm in}n^{\rm in}_m(x)+r_m^{\rm out}\hat\ell_m(x)$. \Cref{app:protocol} gives the features, regularization and calibration.

\section{Experimental Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
The main pool has five routes, called through OpenRouter: gpt-oss-20b at low and medium effort~\citep{gptoss}, deepseek-v4-flash~\citep{deepseekv4}, and gpt-oss-120b at medium and high effort. Endpoints of the same open-weight model differ across providers in accuracy, length and price~\citep{samemodel,gateways}, so we pin deepseek-v4-flash, whose output length depends on the provider, to one provider; the gpt-oss routes are served by whichever provider OpenRouter selects. The three main datasets are LiveCodeBench~\citep{lcb}, Omni-MATH~\citep{omni} and MMLU-Pro~\citep{mmlupro}, with 341, 1,000 and 6,500 test problems; six further datasets are AIME~\citep{aime}, APPS~\citep{apps}, BigCodeBench~\citep{bigcodebench}, CodeContests~\citep{codecontests}, SuperGPQA~\citep{supergpqa} and BIG-Bench Extra Hard~\citep{bbeh}. Each is split into training, calibration and test problems (\cref{app:pools,app:protocol}). On the three main datasets we also extend the pool with three reasoning models from other families (\cref{app:families}). \Cref{tab:routes} gives each route's accuracy and cost on the three main test sets.

\paragraph{Prices.}
Each model has one price per input and per output token: its average billed price, a least-squares fit of the billed cost of our calls on their tokens (USD per million output tokens: 0.13 for gpt-oss-20b, 0.08 for deepseek-v4-flash, 0.26 for gpt-oss-120b; for the gpt-oss models, an average over the providers OpenRouter chose). A call's cost is its tokens at these prices, except on the Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro test sets, where it is the billed cost of the call. Neither choice matters: fitting the prices on training calls only, or costing those two test sets as tokens, moves our saving relative to median pricing by at most 1.0 point, and no comparison in \cref{tab:fresh} flips sign or loses significance (\cref{app:protocol}).

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
Every estimator is fitted on training problems only and evaluated on the test set, with the same success predictions. Sweeping $V$ gives one policy per value and a (cost, accuracy) point on the test set; an estimator's frontier is the upper concave hull of its points, each of which is achievable by sending each query to one of two policies at random with fixed probabilities~\citep{routerbench}. We compare two cost estimators $a,b$ at 12 accuracies that both frontiers reach and report $1-\exp(\overline{\log C_a/C_b})$, the cost saved by $a$ at matched accuracy. An oracle that prices each problem at its realized output length bounds every estimator; its saving over median pricing is the \emph{headroom}, and our share of it the \emph{capture}. Every result is a mean over ten \emph{training sets}, each of which reshuffles the training and calibration problems, keeps the test set and refits everything; intervals are paired bootstrap intervals over test problems, pooled over the ten. Analyses marked ``one training set'' use one split (\cref{app:protocol}). \Cref{sec:deploy} checks the results without interpolation, with $V$ fixed in advance on calibration.

\section{Results}
\paragraph{Cost probes beat every estimator.}
At matched accuracy, prefill cost probes spend 27.8\% less than median-length pricing on LCB, 31.5\% less on Omni-MATH and 23.4\% less on MMLU-Pro (\cref{tab:fresh}), 69\%, 61\% and 40\% of the headroom. Every external estimator loses, by 14--34 points, with intervals excluding zero except ZeroRouter on Omni-MATH, and ours is significantly ahead of each in every training set on all three test sets except in 3 of the 10 Omni-MATH sets against ZeroRouter. A text-embedding model of the prefill's own size loses by 12--23 points under every probe (\cref{tab:textemb}).

\paragraph{Other datasets.}
On the other six datasets (\cref{tab:pools}) our probes save 14.9\% [10.8, 20.1] on AIME, 16.8\% [4.2, 30.4] on APPS, 33.0\% [14.6, 45.7] on SuperGPQA and 23.5\% [9.4, 35.2] on BIG-Bench Extra Hard relative to median pricing; \cref{sec:when} treats BigCodeBench and CodeContests, where they save little. No external estimator is significantly cheaper than ours on any dataset (\cref{tab:allest}), also on random re-splits that reshuffle the test problems (\cref{tab:resplit}).

\paragraph{Headroom is large wherever reasoning lengths vary.}
On eight of nine datasets perfect per-query cost knowledge would save 29--61\%, and on seven of them our probes capture two fifths to over two thirds of it (.40--.69). BigCodeBench has little to capture (10.0\%), and CodeContests has headroom that no estimator reaches (\cref{sec:when}). The headroom also knows the sampling noise of the scored draws, which no predictor can, so the capture figures are conservative (\cref{app:mechanism}).

\paragraph{The saving is not only a price gap.}
Part of the saving could come from prices alone, by sending queries from an expensive route to a cheap one. Keeping every call's realized output length and charging every route the same price per token, our probes still save 13.3\% (LCB), 14.3\% (Omni-MATH) and 36.3\% (MMLU-Pro) relative to median pricing (one training set). On MMLU-Pro this is more than at billed prices (28.8\% on the same training set), because there length differs more between questions than between routes. Price gaps larger than the billed ones shrink both the headroom and our saving: when prices differ by two orders of magnitude, choosing the model dominates, as for software-engineering agents (\cref{app:resplit,app:agents}).

\paragraph{Which routes the saving needs.}
Refitting the router on route subsets (\cref{app:subsets}) locates the saving in a cheap route paired with a strong route whose length varies widely. Without gpt-oss-20b at low effort, the cheapest route, most of it disappears (LCB 27.8\% to 6.5\%, Omni-MATH 31.5\% to 16.2\%), while that route with deepseek-v4-flash alone saves 33.8\% on Omni-MATH, more than the full pool; choosing effort within one model is worth little. deepseek-v4-flash has the widest length spread of any route on all nine datasets, and per-query cost decides, query by query, whether it is worth calling instead of the cheap route.

\paragraph{Reasoning makes per-query cost worth predicting.}
To test whether this is a property of reasoning models, we collected five non-reasoning routes on the same problems (deepseek-v4-flash with thinking disabled, Llama-3.1-8B~\citep{llama3}, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct, Llama-3.3-70B and Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct; \cref{app:settings}) and fitted the probes as for the reasoning routes. With reasoning routes the headroom is 1.3--2.3$\times$ larger, and our saving 2.3$\times$ (LCB) to about 30$\times$ (MMLU-Pro) larger (\cref{tab:nonreason}); on MMLU-Pro the non-reasoning saving is not distinguishable from zero in any training set, and no other estimator does better on Omni-MATH or MMLU-Pro (\cref{app:nonreason}). Much of the non-reasoning variation is degenerate output the prompt predicts poorly, such as Llama-3.1-8B looping until the 16k cap on 9--12\% of math problems. Thinking on deepseek-v4-flash shows the trade directly: it gains 2.8 (MMLU-Pro), 14.6 (Omni-MATH) and 22.1 (LCB) points of accuracy for 13$\times$, 11$\times$ and 33$\times$ the output, and the problems that are long with thinking are long without it (correlation of log length .66--.76).

\section{What the cost probe captures}\label{sec:mechanism}
\paragraph{Length is mostly a property of the problem.}
Fitted on the training problems, the prefill explains much of each route's log output length on the test problems ($R^2$ .71--.75 on LCB, .66--.73 on Omni-MATH, .41--.51 on AIME and .26--.62 on MMLU-Pro; \cref{tab:bracket}). On LCB, Omni-MATH and AIME most of this is shared with difficulty: a problem's empirical solve rate across routes alone reaches $R^2$ .48--.65, and the prefill misses at most .07 of what difficulty explains (.10--.22 on AIME; cross-validated within the test problems, one training set). Within a problem, failed and solved draws are about equally long ($\times$0.83--1.16), except that deepseek-v4-flash writes 2.5$\times$ (Omni-MATH) and 1.9$\times$ (AIME) longer when it fails on hard math. Where pricing from the success probes matches the dedicated probe, it is not because difficulty alone sets length: the true solve rate prices far worse than the success logits, which carry length cues beyond it (\cref{app:mechanism}).

\paragraph{What the data fix, without any predictor.}
Three quantities bound what any estimator could read, without fitting one (\cref{app:legibility}). The noise ceiling, the share of a route's log-length variance fixed by the problem rather than by sampling, is .71--.99 for every reasoning route on every dataset with repeated draws. Other routes' realized lengths on the same problem explain .42--.96 of a route's log length (every route on every dataset except one route on BIG-Bench Extra Hard): length is largely a property of the question that models agree on. The route-specific part of length is small (8--29\% of its variance, against 42--84\% for the level shared across routes) but, within a point, carries as much routing value as the shared level or more: an oracle that knows only the route-specific part saves 24.8\%, 41.4\% and 43.7\% relative to median pricing on LCB, Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro, against 25.5\%, 23.3\% and 25.4\% for one that knows only the shared level (\cref{tab:decomp}).

\paragraph{Where our probe falls short.}
Our probe is the best pre-generation length predictor we have on every route of every dataset (\cref{tab:bracket}), so its $R^2$ bounds how predictable length is from below, and the noise ceiling bounds it from above for any estimator. The two are close on LCB, Omni-MATH and BIG-Bench Extra Hard (e.g.\ LCB .71--.75 against .98--.99) and far apart on the knowledge datasets, above all for deepseek-v4-flash: on MMLU-Pro and SuperGPQA we read .26 and .22 of its log length, other routes' lengths explain .62 and .46, and the ceiling is .92 and .96. Route differences are what predictors read worst (our $R^2$ for a pair's log length ratio is .06--.47 per dataset, against a ceiling of .48--.85), and this differential $R^2$ tracks how much of a pair's headroom we capture (\cref{app:legibility}).

\paragraph{Where length tracks work rather than difficulty.}
On a 300-problem test split of MMLU-Pro (one training set), difficulty explains log length only in part (test $R^2$ .18--.37), subject labels about as much (.17--.25), and the two together .34--.44, against .41--.66 for the prefill; engineering and law questions, for example, are both long (6.4k and 4.1k output tokens on average) and hard. Pricing from difficulty and subject together saves 32.6\%, against 43.9\% for the prefill (headroom 61.5\%): the prefill reads how much work a question requires, beyond its difficulty and topic. On the full test sets, pricing from our success probes, by ridge on the success logits or by ten difficulty bins on them, comes within a few points of the dedicated probe on LCB and Omni-MATH, where length tracks difficulty, and loses by 18.4\% [11.0, 23.5] and 16.7\% [10.8, 21.8] on MMLU-Pro (\cref{tab:allest}). The same holds on BIG-Bench Extra Hard, whose 23 tasks differ in format: there the dedicated probe saves 22.6\% [12.3, 31.9] relative to pricing from success, which saves only 1.2\% over median pricing (\cref{tab:pools}). We added it and SuperGPQA after a screen for this contrast (\cref{app:prereg}).


\section{How cheaply can a router get it?}\label{sec:general}
Where length tracks difficulty, pricing from the success probes ties the dedicated cost probe when both are fitted on a benchmark's full training set (\cref{tab:pools}). A deployed router rarely has that: it has few labels, it adds models, and it meets benchmarks it was not trained on. We test the cost estimators in all three settings, and on one mixture of all nine benchmarks; every estimator gets the same success predictions unless stated, and \cref{app:settings} gives each protocol.

\paragraph{Few labels.}
\Cref{tab:labels} refits every cost estimator on 20 random training problems. Our probe keeps 51--81\% of its full-data saving on every dataset, while the external estimators save less than 10\% on every dataset. Their deficit narrows with data but stays large on most datasets: at 100 problems ours saves 17.9\% on MMLU-Pro against at most 6.5\% for any external estimator, with every paired interval excluding zero. The exception is BIG-Bench Extra Hard, where the task format is visible in the text: the text estimators reach 15--17\% at 100 problems and tie ours at full data. Pricing from the success probes matches or exceeds ours on LCB and Omni-MATH from 20 problems on (22.5\% and 26.6\%), but stays below 7\% at every $n$ on MMLU-Pro and BIG-Bench Extra Hard, where the dedicated probe keeps climbing (\cref{fig:labels}). Labelling one sampled output per training problem instead of all of them gives up 1--3 points (one training set).

\paragraph{New models.}
To add a model, a router needs its success and cost probes. We hold out one model at a time, with all of its efforts, and fit its probes from $k$ labelled problems while every other route keeps its full probes. Our onboarding fits two parameters per probe: an offset to the other routes' predicted log length, and a logistic map from their mean success logit. We compare it with ZeroRouter's onboarding, its main use case~\citep{zerorouter}, and with a CARROT-style nearest-neighbour arm. From 10 examples our router saves 19.2--26.7\% on four datasets and 9.7\% on BIG-Bench Extra Hard, against 3.4--17.4\% for ZeroRouter's onboarding (\cref{tab:onboard}). It leads on every dataset on average (by 5.2--13.0 points), significantly at $k$=50 on LCB and SuperGPQA, and the gain concentrates on deepseek-v4-flash, the one model whose family is not otherwise in the pool.

\paragraph{Three further model families.}
Adding Qwen3-32B, GLM-4.7-Flash and Nemotron-3-Super-120B on LCB, Omni-MATH and a 2,700-problem subset of MMLU-Pro leaves the picture unchanged (\cref{app:families}). Without deepseek-v4-flash, in a pool of both gpt-oss-20b efforts and the three new models, our probes still save 29.5\% [17.3, 37.2], 12.4\% [$-$0.2, 21.7] and 22.7\% [9.9, 33.6] relative to median pricing: the gain does not rest on one cheap and accurate route.

\paragraph{Mixed traffic.}

\section{When it works and when it does not}\label{sec:when}
Per-query cost pays under two conditions: (a) output length varies within a route by more than prices differ between routes, which creates headroom, and (b) some estimator can predict that variation from the prompt, which captures it. Condition (b) concerns every possible estimator, so we bound it from above by the noise ceiling, which holds for any estimator, and from below by the best estimator we tried. For every reasoning route here the ceiling is high (.71--.99): the variation is fixed by the problem, and whether it is read depends on the estimator. Each of our two failures violates one condition for every estimator we tried (\cref{fig:capture}).

\paragraph{Little headroom: BigCodeBench.}
BigCodeBench tasks are library-usage functions with short, similar solutions, and perfect cost knowledge saves only 10.0\% [$-$1.1, 20.1]. No estimator's saving relative to median pricing has an interval excluding zero (ours 0.4 [$-$9.5, 9.1], ZeroRouter 8.5 [$-$13.0, 26.5], MixLLM-style, pricing from success and prompt features $-$2.0 to $-$1.1). Length here is the least tied to difficulty of any dataset, but there is little to gain from reading it.

\paragraph{Headroom no estimator reads: CodeContests.}
CodeContests has as much headroom as APPS (35.8\% [26.5, 43.7]), but no estimator captures more than about a quarter of it: ours saves 9.4\% [0.4, 19.8] and difficulty bins 7.7\% [0.1, 18.1], barely excluding zero, and every other estimator is within noise of zero. Length again tracks difficulty, but difficulty is hard to read from these prompts: the prefill misses .11--.18 of the length $R^2$ that empirical difficulty explains, against at most .07 on LCB and Omni-MATH (one training set). The lengths are fixed by the problem (noise ceiling .92--.96) and shared across routes (other routes' lengths explain .67--.88), against our $R^2$ of .35--.52: the shortfall is in reading them from these prompts, for every estimator we tried.

\paragraph{When the text alone suffices: BIG-Bench Extra Hard.}
The converse case also occurs. BIG-Bench Extra Hard has 23 tasks with very different formats, and the task is visible in the text, so text-only estimators read length as well as the prefill: MixLLM-style embeddings and prompt features save 24.4\% and 21.9\% relative to median pricing (ours $-$1.3 [$-$5.1, 2.7] and 2.1 [$-$3.8, 7.0] relative to them). On SuperGPQA, the other dataset of mixed subjects, they do not (ours saves 15.7\% [0.1, 27.5] and 19.1\% [3.1, 32.2] relative to them).

\section{Controls}\label{sec:ablations}
\paragraph{A text-embedding model of the same size.}
\Cref{tab:textemb} reads cost from Qwen3-Embedding-4B~\citep{qwen3emb}, a text-embedding model with the prefill's parameter count, with three probes (our ridge recipe, a MixLLM-style ensemble and CARROT-style $k$-nearest neighbours) and our success predictions, and builds a fully text-based router that also predicts success from the embedding. On LCB, Omni-MATH, MMLU-Pro and AIME our probe saves 9.7--23.1\% relative to every embedding cost probe and 6.8--18.5\% relative to the text router, with intervals excluding zero; on the other five datasets it is ahead of every embedding cost probe on average and never significantly behind any arm. This does not charge the embedding its own encoder pass, which would only favour our probes.

\paragraph{Other prefills.}
Any small prefill works (\cref{tab:size}): every Qwen3 size from 0.6B to 4B saves 17.0--20.6\% on MMLU-Pro and 27.9--29.0\% on Omni-MATH relative to median pricing with its own success probes, and prefills from three other families (Phi-4-mini~\citep{phi4mini}, Granite-3.3-2B, SmolLM2-1.7B~\citep{smollm2}) save 16.9--21.4\% and 25.1--28.2\%. Ours is ahead of each by 2.0--8.2 points on average, significantly only against three of them on MMLU-Pro.

\paragraph{Is fine-tuning needed?}
Fully fine-tuning DistilBERT 66M, jina-embeddings 137M or Qwen3-0.6B on the same length labels, as CARROT does with RoBERTa~\citep{carrot}, gives a cost estimator that saves less than our probe on Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro (by 11.9--25.6\%, one training set) and on LCB (by 6.5\% [$-$1.2, 12.2] for Qwen3-0.6B, ten training sets). The same Qwen3-0.6B, frozen and read by a linear probe, saves more than its fine-tuned copy (\cref{app:size}): the length information is already in a pretrained decoder's prefill.

\paragraph{Is the prefill router's success pipeline needed?}
Our per-route logistic probes on fixed layers tie a reimplementation of the prefill router's success pipeline~\citep{prefill} (per-target layer, pooling and PCA selection, then an MLP ensemble) on LCB, Omni-MATH, MMLU-Pro and BIG-Bench Extra Hard, and trail it on SuperGPQA, where success is barely predictable for either (one training set; \cref{app:size}). They are cheaper to fit, and the same features feed both probes.

\section{Deployment}\label{sec:deploy}
\paragraph{Routing versus single models.}
Any point on the convex hull of the routes' cost--accuracy points can be reached without a router, by sending each query to a route drawn at random with fixed probabilities; this mix is the baseline a router has to beat~\citep{routerbench}. At matched accuracy our router spends 37.3\% [29.5, 44.3], 39.2\% [30.5, 45.8] and 22.9\% [13.5, 28.8] less than the mix on LCB, Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro and 14.9--46.6\% less on AIME, SuperGPQA and BIG-Bench Extra Hard, and ties it on APPS, BigCodeBench and CodeContests. With median pricing, the same success probes do not beat the mix on MMLU-Pro ($-$0.7\% [$-$5.7, 2.8]) or AIME (0.0\%): there the cost estimate decides whether routing pays at all.

The router does not beat the best single model at that model's accuracy. On every dataset deepseek-v4-flash is the most accurate route or within 1.6 points of it, at 17--37\% of the cost of gpt-oss-120b at high effort; the router's most accurate policy ends 0.2--2.7 points below the most accurate route on seven datasets, and where it reaches deepseek-v4-flash's accuracy it spends at least as much as calling it alone. Below the top, giving up five points of accuracy saves 14.9--40.3\% relative to calling deepseek-v4-flash alone on six datasets, 1.5--1.8$\times$ what median pricing saves at the same accuracy. The shortfall at the top recurs without deepseek-v4-flash or gpt-oss-120b in the pool (\cref{app:families}), and per-query budgets change it little: under a hard cap on each call's cost, the router beats deepseek-v4-flash alone only at the tightest cap we tested (by 8.2 and 7.0 points of accuracy on LCB and MMLU-Pro), and ties or trails it by up to 3 points at looser caps (\cref{app:deploy}).

\paragraph{Deployable policies.}
Our main metric interpolates on test-set frontiers; a deployed router must fix $V$ in advance. For each estimator and each of ten accuracy targets, we choose the cheapest $V$ that reaches the target on calibration and apply it once to the test set (\cref{tab:deploy}). Our deployed policies save 19.6--37.9\% relative to the median rule's on LCB, Omni-MATH, MMLU-Pro, AIME, SuperGPQA and BIG-Bench Extra Hard, cost 1.0--3.3\% more than our own test frontier at the accuracy they reach, and miss their target by 2.2--5.3 points. They lead the best deployed external estimator on LCB, Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro in every training set, on AIME in 9 and on SuperGPQA in 7 of 10, and tie it on BIG-Bench Extra Hard (\cref{app:deploy}).

\paragraph{The router's own cost.}
The features come from one forward pass of Qwen3-4B over the prompt (19--28\,ms median latency on one H100), which a router that predicts success from the prefill already pays; the cost probe adds nothing to it. Against no routing at all the pass is not free, because the routed models are cheap: self-hosted at an assumed \$2 per H100-hour it costs 2--10\% of our deployed saving on most datasets and about 30\% on the two with long prompts (\cref{app:deploy}).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\paragraph{When does per-query cost pay?}
It pays when output length varies within a model more than prices differ between models: here lengths span 11--121$\times$ within a route on LCB while per-call costs span 17--30$\times$ across routes, leaving 40--58\% headroom over median pricing on the three main datasets. The saving comes from deciding, query by query, whether a strong route whose cost varies widely is worth calling instead of a cheap one. For reasoning routes the variation is mostly fixed by the problem, so what limits capture is the estimator, most of all on route differences and on knowledge questions. Where length tracks difficulty, even difficulty-only pricing captures most of it; where it tracks the work a question requires, only a dedicated probe does, and that is also where we capture least (40\% on MMLU-Pro), leaving the most room for better cost predictors. The same logic extends to software engineering: one-shot patch generation behaves like the coding datasets, while for agents, whose prices span two orders of magnitude, choosing the model dominates (\cref{app:agents}).

\paragraph{Generalisation.}
In-domain and with full training data, a router could price from difficulty and lose little on most datasets. The case for a dedicated cost probe is that it holds up where a deployed router actually operates: with few labels, with new models, on new benchmarks and in mixed traffic, where both difficulty-only pricing and text estimators lose most of their saving (\cref{sec:general}). A benchmark unlike its sources still needs some labels: on Omni-MATH and BIG-Bench Extra Hard the transferred router saves little.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Each route needs output-length labels, though a few dozen suffice for most datasets. At the top of the frontier the router does not beat the most accurate single route, because its success predictions are imperfect. Telling apart two strong routes of similar price is the open part of the problem: billing gpt-oss-120b at high effort at deepseek-v4-flash's token rates, perfect per-query cost knowledge would save 11--36\% within that pair, but our probes save at most 7.0\% [2.4, 14.0] (Omni-MATH) relative to calling deepseek-v4-flash alone at its accuracy. The saving also needs a cheap route that solves a good share of problems. The main experiments use five routes from two model families, with three further families on three datasets, and transfer is evaluated across benchmarks that share the routed models. Intervals are pointwise and assume independent problems, and a few analyses use one training set. The Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro test sets have one draw per problem and route. Of the five main routes only deepseek-v4-flash is pinned to a provider; billed rates come from one collection, and prices change within days. The ZeroRouter, MixLLM and CARROT baselines are reimplementations.

\section{Conclusion}
A frozen prefill that already predicts success also predicts cost. Per-query cost is worth predicting for reasoning models, whose lengths vary within a model by more than prices differ between models, and much less for non-reasoning ones. On held-out test problems at billed prices, prefill cost probes reduce spending at matched accuracy and outperform every alternative estimator we tested; pricing from difficulty alone matches them only where output length tracks difficulty and only with full in-domain data. With few labels and for new models the dedicated probe keeps most of its saving, and on four of seven new benchmarks it does so without any target labels; in mixed traffic it adds a tenth of the cost beyond what knowing each query's benchmark and difficulty saves.


\section*{Reproducibility}
Every number in the paper is computed from stored per-call records (prompt, response, grade, token counts, billed cost and serving provider) and stored prefill activations, with every split fixed by a seed; no analysis issues a new model call. \Cref{app:protocol} gives the evaluation protocol, \cref{app:baselines} the reimplemented estimators, and \cref{app:pools} the datasets, splits and billed rates. Collection used OpenRouter with deepseek-v4-flash pinned to one provider; prices and provider behaviour change over time, so rerunning collection will not reproduce billed costs exactly, while every analysis reproduces from the released records.

\section{Implementation details}\label{app:protocol}
\subsection{Data, routes and labels}
All calls go through OpenRouter. deepseek-v4-flash is pinned to StreamLake by restricting OpenRouter's provider routing to that provider with fallbacks disabled; the gpt-oss routes are served by whichever provider OpenRouter selects. LiveCodeBench has 892 problems (contests from September 2023 to April 2025), split at random into 441 training, 110 calibration and 341 test problems. Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro have 275/75 and 550/150 training/calibration problems, from the 500-problem Omni-MATH set and a 1,000-question subject-stratified sample of MMLU-Pro, and 1,000 and 6,500 disjoint test problems. The six further datasets are split at random (\cref{tab:poolstats}). Training problems have 2--16 draws per route; Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro test problems have one. Every draw is scored by the benchmark's own grader (code execution against the tests for the coding datasets; answer matching for the others), and evaluated problems need a valid answer from every route.

\subsection{Prices}
Effective rates are least-squares fits of each call's billed cost on its input and output tokens, per model (\cref{tab:poolstats}). For the gpt-oss models they average over the providers OpenRouter chose, which billed gpt-oss-120b from 0.17 to 0.60 USD per million output tokens. A problem's realized cost on a route is the mean billed cost of its valid draws on the Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro test sets, and the mean of its valid draws' input and output tokens at the effective rates elsewhere. Two checks over ten training sets: fitting the rates on training calls only (the same for deepseek-v4-flash and gpt-oss-120b, a 15\% higher output price for gpt-oss-20b), and costing the Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro test sets as tokens at the effective rates. Each moves our saving relative to median pricing by at most 1.0 point, and no comparison in \cref{tab:fresh} flips sign or loses significance. SuperGPQA and BIG-Bench Extra Hard were collected later, when deepseek-v4-flash was billed at about twice its earlier rate; we price them like every other dataset (at their own billed rates our saving there would be 4.9 and 1.1 points higher, on one training set).

\subsection{Probes}
The features are the mean- and last-token activations of eight layers of Qwen3-4B (Instruct-2507; Thinking-2507 on Omni-MATH), concatenated and shifted and scaled to zero mean and unit variance on the training problems. Success probes are per-route L2 logistic regressions, with the regularization chosen on calibration AUC from nine values and Platt scaling fitted on calibration. Cost probes are per-route ridge regressions of log mean output length, with the regularization chosen by cross-validation within the training problems; the exponentiated prediction is corrected by Duan's smearing from the training residuals and its level matched to the route's training mean.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\paragraph{Frontiers and matched accuracy.} Each value $V$ on a geometric grid of 300 values gives a deterministic policy (\cref{eq:route}) and a test point (mean realized cost, mean accuracy). An arm's frontier is the upper concave hull of its points, and its cost at a target accuracy is linearly interpolated on the hull. Two arms are compared at 12 accuracies evenly spaced over the interior 90\% of the accuracy range both frontiers reach; the reported saving is one minus the geometric mean of their cost ratios. Which problems are used for training matters at these sizes, so every result is a mean over ten training sets. A training set reshuffles the training and calibration problems (with a fixed seed per training set) into splits of the same sizes and keeps the test problems; the success probes and every estimator are refitted and every configuration is re-chosen on its calibration problems. Within a training set, intervals resample test problems with replacement (300 paired resamples, the same for both arms and for every training set, all routes of a problem kept together) with that set's probes fixed; reported intervals are percentiles of the 3,000 resamples pooled over the ten training sets, so they reflect both the choice of training problems and the test problems, and reported values are means of the ten point estimates.
\paragraph{Analyses on one training set.} Every table and every number in the main text is a mean over the ten training sets unless marked ``one training set''. Those marked use one fixed split of each dataset and compare arms within it: held-out draws, rescaled prices, the within-test variance decomposition and length drivers of \cref{sec:mechanism}, fine-tuned encoders on Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro, parts of the transfer analysis, the prefill router's success pipeline, the perfect-judge cascade and the software-engineering datasets.

\subsection{Reimplemented estimators}\label{app:baselines}
Every estimator predicts each route's log mean output length from information available before generation, is fitted on the training problems only, and is post-processed like our probe (residual smearing, level matched to the route's training mean); configurations are chosen on the calibration split. \emph{Prompt-feature GBM} follows latency-aware routing~\citep{latencyrouting}: histogram gradient boosting (300 iterations, learning rate 0.05, minimum leaf 10) on 44 prompt statistics (length, line count, count and magnitude of numbers, math delimiters, examples, constraint markers, code fences, inequality signs and 34 keyword counts), with scikit-learn's histogram boosting in place of LightGBM. \emph{MixLLM-style}~\citep{mixllm}: a jina-embeddings-v2 137M embedding~\citep{jina} of the prompt (the code variant on LiveCodeBench), read by the average of an MLP, a random forest and a $k$-nearest-neighbour regressor per route; MixLLM used a BERT-family encoder. \emph{CARROT-style $k$NN}~\citep{carrot}: the authors' local variant, MiniLM-L12~\citep{minilm} sentence embeddings with uniform cosine $k$NN, $k$ chosen by five-fold cross-validation on training problems. \emph{ZeroRouter}~\citep{zerorouter}: stage 1 fits a $D$-dimensional two-parameter item-response model to the five routes' training outcomes (MAP with Gaussian priors; the paper uses stochastic variational inference over about 200 leaderboard models); stage 2 regresses each problem's item parameters $(\log\alpha, b)$ on our 4B prefill features (PCA to 256 dimensions, ridge), a stronger reader on our datasets than its fine-tuned DistilBERT~\citep{distilbert}, which we also ran; cost is the route's mean training length within $K$ quantile bins of $s=\alpha^\top b$. $D\in\{1,5\}$ and $K\in\{5,10,20\}$ are chosen on calibration. With five routes a higher-dimensional latent does not improve its success predictions, and on MMLU-Pro adding up to 53 Open LLM Leaderboard models to stage 1 did not either. In a component swap during development, ZeroRouter's success model paired with our cost probe came close to our router, while our success probes paired with its bin pricing did not: its shortfall is in the pricing. The fine-tuned encoders of \cref{sec:ablations} are mean-pooled with a linear head on per-route standardised log length, trained with masked squared error (AdamW, 6\% warm-up then linear decay, maximum 512 tokens for DistilBERT and 2,048 for the others); learning rate (two values per encoder) and epoch (up to 10, or 20 when the best epoch was the last) are chosen on calibration error, and smearing and level match use the calibration problems.

\subsection{Experiment settings}\label{app:settings}
\paragraph{Non-reasoning routes.} deepseek-v4-flash with thinking disabled uses the same provider and sampling as with thinking; the four other non-reasoning models are each pinned to one provider. Every non-reasoning route has one draw per problem, with output capped at 16k tokens. Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct has training answers for 241 of 275 Omni-MATH and 485 of 550 MMLU-Pro problems (rate limits).
\paragraph{Few labels.} Each cost estimator is refitted on $n$ random training problems, two subsets in each of the ten training sets, with the same success probes for every arm. ZeroRouter pricing fits its item-response latent on the $n$ problems, reads it from the prefill and uses its bin lookup; the $k$NN neighbour count and ZeroRouter's dimension and bin count are chosen on calibration. Ours exceeds every external estimator with paired intervals excluding zero on LCB and Omni-MATH from $n$=20 (except Omni-MATH at $n$=50), on MMLU-Pro from $n$=100 and on SuperGPQA from $n$=200. On BIG-Bench Extra Hard the text estimators stay within noise of ours at every $n$; below 100 problems ours leads on average (by 16.5 points over MixLLM-style embeddings at 50), with intervals that include zero.
\paragraph{Onboarding.} The held-out model is gpt-oss-20b (both efforts), gpt-oss-120b (both efforts) or deepseek-v4-flash, so that no sibling effort remains in the pool, with four random choices of the $k$ anchors in each training set, the same in every arm. ZeroRouter's onboarding fits the new model's ability on the $k$ anchors in an item-response latent fitted on the remaining routes and fills its per-bin length table from them~\citep{zerorouter}; the CARROT-style arm averages the anchors' outcomes and lengths over nearest neighbours. ZeroRouter's dimension and bin count and the $k$NN neighbour count are chosen on calibration.
\paragraph{Transfer.} The domains are coding (LCB, APPS, BigCodeBench, CodeContests) and math and knowledge (Omni-MATH, MMLU-Pro, AIME, SuperGPQA, BIG-Bench Extra Hard). Every choice (regularization, Platt scaling, neighbour counts, latent dimension, bin count) is made on the source datasets' calibration splits, and each training set reshuffles every dataset's splits with the same seed. External estimators are transferred the same way, each with its own success model (MixLLM-style: a logistic probe on the text embedding; $k$NN; ZeroRouter: its latent and bins). Omni-MATH enters as its 500-problem set (test 150), whose prefill matches the other datasets'. BigCodeBench and CodeContests, where no in-domain estimator saves significantly, are not used as targets: there the transferred success probes are badly miscalibrated (BigCodeBench log loss 1.17 against .67 in-domain) and the apparent gains are not robust.
\paragraph{Mixed traffic.} Training and calibration problems are the union of each dataset's splits; the test set has 150 problems from each dataset (1,350); Omni-MATH enters as its 500-problem set. Both probes are refitted on the mixture, and bootstrap resamples are drawn within each benchmark.

\section{Non-reasoning routes}\label{app:nonreason}
Adding the non-reasoning routes to the reasoning pool lowers our saving (14.8\% on Omni-MATH, 7.6\% on MMLU-Pro, 19.2\% on LCB). Non-reasoning lengths do vary (s.d.\ of log length .53--1.73 per route), but the variation has two sources that the prompt predicts poorly. Llama-3.1-8B, at its default sampling settings, falls into repetition until the 16k cap on 9--12\% of math problems (four in five capped answers loop), so its mean output is 4--6$\times$ its median and its log-length $R^2$ is below zero on every set. This is the model rather than the provider: redrawn on 300 such problems, it loops again on 29\% with the same provider and 19\% with another, against about 5\% on control problems for both; a repetition penalty of 1.1 removes most loops (6\%), apparently at some cost in accuracy. Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct, asked for code only on LCB, reasons inside code comments on hard problems until the cap (9\% of problems), so its lengths span 142$\times$ between its 10th- and 90th-percentile problems ($R^2$ .27): the largest non-reasoning headroom comes from a non-reasoning model that reasons anyway.

\paragraph{Every estimator on non-reasoning routes.}
Non-reasoning routes combine both failures (\cref{tab:nonreason}): less headroom, and on Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro a capture of .17 and .03. No other estimator does better. Fitting every estimator of \cref{tab:allest,tab:textemb} on the non-reasoning routes (ten training sets), none of mean length, prompt features, MixLLM-style or nearest-neighbour embeddings, ZeroRouter's bins and the three Qwen3-Embedding-4B probes saves significantly relative to median pricing on either dataset (at most 1.9\%); on Omni-MATH ours is ahead of each by 4.8--12.2 points with pricing from success tied, and on MMLU-Pro every estimator, ours included, is within noise of median pricing. Most of their length is nonetheless readable: on Omni-MATH our probe explains .55--.75 of four routes' log length, about what the other non-reasoning routes' lengths on the same problem explain (.48--.70). The exception is Llama-3.1-8B, whose loops on math problems make its mean output 4--6$\times$ its median: no estimator reads its length (best $R^2$ .10--.22 across the three datasets) and neither do other models' lengths (.14--.40). What can be read is worth little here, and the most erratic route cannot be read at all. On LCB the probes capture .53 of a smaller headroom, with pricing from success and the Qwen3-Embedding-4B ridge probe close behind (11.3\% and 10.4\% against 12.1\%).

\section{Further mechanism results}\label{app:mechanism}
All results in this appendix are on one training set.
\paragraph{The headroom is an upper bound.}
Pricing each problem at its realized cost also knows the sampling noise of the draws it is scored on, which no predictor of expected cost can. Where test problems have several draws per route, we split each problem's draws at random into a half that prices and a half that is scored (20 splits). On one training set, pricing at the held-out half's mean length, a target an expected-cost predictor could reach, saves 41.2\% on LiveCodeBench against 45.9\% for the realized-cost oracle on the same halves; the gap is larger where draws are few (AIME 20.1 against 30.1, Omni-MATH-500 23.6 against 35.9, CodeContests 23.4 against 38.9) and small on SuperGPQA (52.7 against 57.1). With one or two draws per half the held-out oracle is itself noisy, so the achievable headroom lies between the two; on LiveCodeBench, where it is tightest, our probes capture .81 of the held-out bound against .72 of the realized one. The capture figures in \cref{tab:pools} are therefore conservative.

\paragraph{Success and cost are read from the same layers.}
Refitting both probes on each stored layer separately, success AUC and length $R^2$ peak in the same late layers at the last token (layers 22--36 of 36), and one such layer matches the full concatenation (LCB AUC .86--.87, $R^2$ .73--.75, saving 33--34\%; Omni-MATH .83--.84, .71--.72, 35--37\%; MMLU-Pro .72, .48--.50, 27--29\%). The two probes are close to one signal where length tracks difficulty: the mean success logit correlates with predicted log length at $-$.90 to $-$.97 per layer on LCB and $-$.71 to $-$.84 on Omni-MATH, but only $-$.36 to $-$.58 on MMLU-Pro.

\paragraph{What changes in the routing.}
At matched accuracy in the middle of the band, 31--66\% of problems change route relative to median pricing. The dominant changes are between gpt-oss-20b-low and deepseek-v4-flash: problems predicted to be short move to the more accurate deepseek-v4-flash (19--38\% of problems), and problems predicted to be long move to the cheap route (9--18\%).

\paragraph{The success probes carry more than difficulty.}
Where pricing from success ties the dedicated probe (LCB, Omni-MATH), it does not price by difficulty alone. A problem's true solve rate over the five routes (with its square) is a weak price even in-domain: it saves 21.5\% on LCB against 34.4\% for pricing from the success logits, and nothing on the 500-problem Omni-MATH set ($-$4.1\% against 18.0\%), because the logits carry length cues beyond the solve rate.
\paragraph{The part of predicted length that difficulty does not explain.}
The part of the predicted length that the true solve rates do not explain is real. It correlates with the same part of the realized length at .56--.92 across six datasets (MMLU-Pro .81: engineering questions are predicted 0.95 log units longer than their difficulty implies, and are 0.95 longer). It also predicts the length of five held-out non-reasoning models on MMLU-Pro (partial correlation .40--.71) while saying almost nothing about whether the stronger of them succeed ($|r| \le .05$ for deepseek-v4-flash without thinking and Qwen3-235B-Instruct).

\section{What the data fix, and what our probe reads}\label{app:legibility}
\Cref{tab:bracket} gives, per route, three test $R^2$ values of log mean output length: our probe's, which is the highest of every pre-generation estimator we evaluated (pricing from success, ZeroRouter's latent, prompt features, MixLLM-style and nearest-neighbour probes of the text encoder of \cref{app:baselines}, ridge, MixLLM-style and nearest-neighbour probes of Qwen3-Embedding-4B, and on LCB the fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B) on every route of every dataset; that of a linear map from the mean realized log length of the other four routes on the same problem, which is not a deployable estimator but shows how much of a route's length is shared with other models; and the noise ceiling, the share of the variance of a route's per-problem log length over test problems that is between problems rather than between draws (from the routes' repeated draws; APPS has one draw per problem). Means over ten training sets.

\Cref{tab:decomp} decomposes log length with no predictor. Over problems, routes and draws (problems with a valid draw on every route), after removing route means, a two-way random-effects decomposition (method of moments, harmonic-mean draw count) splits the variance into a shared problem level, a problem-by-route interaction and within-cell noise. Headroom by source compares oracles that know, for each test problem, its realized shared level only (with each route's training-mean offset), its route-specific part only (with the training-mean level), or both (the headroom of the main text); each is level-matched to the route's training mean like the probes.

We fixed four models of headroom in advance, fitted by least squares over the 138 route subsets of \cref{app:subsets} (full pools, leave-one-out subsets and pairs), using only predictor-free statistics: (A) the price spread (s.d.\ over routes of log mean cost per call), (B) the problem and interaction variances, (C) both, and (D) the log ratio of query-driven variance to squared price spread. In-sample $R^2$ is .32, .18, .37 and .11; leave-one-dataset-out $R^2$ is .25, $-$.23, .01 and $-$.01. Headroom depends on which routes solve which problems, not only on how lengths and prices vary.

\paragraph{What our probe reads.}
Our probe is the best pre-generation length predictor we have on every route of every dataset, ahead of pricing from success, ZeroRouter's latent, prompt features, MixLLM-style and nearest-neighbour probes of a text encoder, three probes of Qwen3-Embedding-4B and, on LCB, the fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B (\cref{tab:bracket}). Its $R^2$ is a lower bound on how predictable length is before generation, and the noise ceiling an upper bound that holds for any estimator. The two are close on LCB, Omni-MATH and BIG-Bench Extra Hard (e.g.\ LCB .71--.75 against .98--.99), and far apart on the knowledge datasets, for deepseek-v4-flash above all: on MMLU-Pro and SuperGPQA we read .26 and .22 of its log length, other routes' lengths explain .62 and .46, and the ceiling is .92 and .96. Its length there is fixed by the question and largely shared with other models, but the prefill misses it. Route differences are what predictors read worst: across route pairs, our $R^2$ for the log length ratio is .06--.47 per dataset, against a ceiling of .48--.85, and this differential $R^2$ tracks the share of a pair's headroom we capture (Spearman .49 over the 75 pairs with headroom above 5 points) better than per-route $R^2$ does (.18). On LCB (one training set), the prefill reads the level ($R^2$ .75) far better than the differences (.41), and pricing with the predicted level alone keeps 93\% of the full probe's saving.

\section{Three further model families}\label{app:families}
\paragraph{Three further model families.}
We added Qwen3-32B, GLM-4.7-Flash and Nemotron-3-Super-120B, all reasoning, on LCB, Omni-MATH and a 2,700-problem subset of MMLU-Pro (billed \$0.40--0.57 per million output tokens; Qwen3-32B's provider stops at 24,575 output tokens, and Nemotron has no valid answer for 27\% of Omni-MATH problems after retries), and fitted the same probes for them (test success AUC .72--.87, log-length $R^2$ .23--.81). Averaged over ten training sets, with all eight routes our probes save 28.8\%, 25.6\% and 23.0\% relative to median pricing, beat difficulty bins (15.5 [6.8, 23.2]) and pricing from success (6.7 [0.3, 12.2]) on MMLU-Pro, and tie both on LCB and Omni-MATH, as with five routes. In the full pool the new models are never needed (removing any one changes the saving by under 0.2 points on one training set): deepseek-v4-flash is cheaper than each of them at a higher accuracy. We therefore also route in a pool of both gpt-oss-20b efforts and the three new models, without deepseek-v4-flash or gpt-oss-120b. There our probes save 29.5\% [17.3, 37.2], 12.4\% [$-$0.2, 21.7] and 22.7\% [9.9, 33.6] relative to median pricing on LCB, Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro, beat difficulty bins on LCB (7.9 [0.3, 14.3]) and MMLU-Pro (12.5 [0.9, 23.6]) and tie pricing from success; with all routes except deepseek-v4-flash they save 25.3\%, 11.4\% and 22.4\%. Without deepseek-v4-flash no single route is cheap and accurate: the most accurate one, Nemotron-3-Super-120B (85.2\%, 75.2\% and 82.4\%), costs \$6.6, \$7.5 and \$2.1 per thousand calls against \$0.6, \$0.9 and \$0.2 for gpt-oss-20b at medium effort; our router's most accurate policy ends 1.6--3.2 points below it. With gpt-oss-120b in the pool, the router reaches Nemotron's accuracy on LCB at 64.8\% [42.3, 83.8] lower cost. Onboarding is tested in the pool of cheap routes (ten training sets, 20 random choices of the $k$ anchors in each). There only Nemotron matters (without it the saving falls from 29.5\% to 21.1\% on LCB, 12.4\% to 10.5\% on Omni-MATH and 22.7\% to 8.1\% on MMLU-Pro). Finally (one training set), the new models' probes trained on Omni-MATH and used on MMLU-Pro keep the full saving (28.5\% against 28.6\% in-domain).

\section{Generalisation details}\label{app:transfer}
\paragraph{Onboarding anchors.} Over the ten training sets of \cref{tab:onboard}, ZeroRouter's Fisher-information anchors help both methods about equally; with the same anchors ours ties on LCB and Omni-MATH and leads by 3.5--12.9 points on the other three datasets at $k$=10.
\paragraph{Combining source and target labels.} The remaining results in this appendix are on one training set. Combining both sources of labels is better than either: with ten labelled target problems added to the source data, the cost probe saves 27.9\% on MMLU-Pro and 13.1\% on AIME (with the target's success probes), against 8.1\% and 4.4\% from the ten problems alone, and 24.1\% against 14.7\% on BIG-Bench Extra Hard with twenty. 
\paragraph{Transferring pricing from success.} Pricing from the success probes transfers unevenly. With the target's own success probes and a map trained on the other datasets, it keeps its saving where the target's kind of difficulty resembles its sources' (APPS 17.1\%, AIME 12.3\%, CodeContests 9.3\%) and collapses elsewhere: from 34.4\% to $-$6.2\% on LCB, 19.6\% to $-$21.9\% on SuperGPQA, 18.0\% to 6.5\% on Omni-MATH and 5.5\% to $-$20.5\% on BIG-Bench Extra Hard. Under the same conditions the dedicated probe saves 30.2\%, 17.7\%, 15.7\% and 16.1\% on those four, and ZeroRouter's difficulty-bin pricing transfers as unevenly as pricing from success.
\paragraph{Which estimators lose least.}
Holding the target's success probes fixed, we bootstrap over test problems the drop from in-domain to transferred pricing for each estimator, and its difference from our drop. Ours loses significantly less than pricing from success on LCB (37.1 points [26.9, 46.0]), SuperGPQA (31.8 [16.3, 49.9]) and BIG-Bench Extra Hard (13.4 [1.4, 23.9]), and less than ZeroRouter's bin pricing on LCB (21.0 [11.7, 28.5]) and MMLU-Pro (4.0 [1.1, 8.2]); on the other datasets the drops are within noise of each other, and on no dataset does ours lose significantly more. Our own drop is significant only on the two heterogeneous datasets (9.8 and 12.5 points).
\paragraph{Why the cost probe transfers.}
Training one probe on the target together with the other datasets matches the target-only probe within 4.4 points on every dataset, with no difference significant, so a single probe can serve all of them. Part of what transfers is shared structure: 24--40\% of each route's variance in log length lies between benchmarks, and the routes agree on which benchmarks are long (mean correlation of benchmark means across routes .84). Length $R^2$ under transfer is close to in-domain on LCB, APPS, AIME, Omni-MATH and SuperGPQA but falls sharply on MMLU-Pro (.02--.46 against .29--.63), where routing transfers fully nonetheless: routing needs the ordering of problems within a route and the contrast between routes, not calibrated absolute lengths. What does not transfer is the map from difficulty to length. Matching the problems of two benchmarks by solve rate (per route, five bins), their lengths still differ by 1.14 log units on average ($\times$3.1 over 470 cells). Our cost probe, trained on neither benchmark, predicts these gaps (correlation .95, 90\% of the squared gap), pricing from success 30\%, and any price from difficulty alone none, by construction. The curve from solve rate to length also differs in shape: it is flat on BigCodeBench, and without BigCodeBench among LCB's sources a linear map from the mean success logit to length transfers to LCB (12.5\% to 32.5\%; in-domain 34.7\%). Difficulty is a benchmark-specific proxy for work; the prefill probe reads cues of the work itself, which carry over when the sources contain the target's kinds of work.

We refit the router on every subset of the five routes that leaves out one route, and on every pair (\cref{tab:subsets}; ten training sets). Choosing effort within one model is worth little: with only gpt-oss-20b's two efforts ours saves 20.2\% (LCB), 16.8\% (Omni-MATH) and 9.0\% (MMLU-Pro) relative to median pricing, with only gpt-oss-120b's 0.2\%, 7.0\% and 6.0\%. \section{Representation and prefill size}\label{app:size}
\paragraph{Representation, probe and prefill size.}
To compare representations at equal size, \cref{tab:textemb} reads cost from Qwen3-Embedding-4B~\citep{qwen3emb}, a text-embedding model with the prefill's parameter count, with three probes (our ridge recipe, a MixLLM-style ensemble and CARROT-style $k$-nearest neighbours) and our success predictions, and builds a fully text-based router that also predicts success from the embedding with our logistic recipe. On LCB, Omni-MATH, MMLU-Pro and AIME our probe saves 9.7--23.1\% relative to every embedding cost probe and 6.8--18.5\% relative to the text router, with intervals excluding zero (ahead in every training set against the cost probes, and in 8--10 of 10 against the text router). On the other five datasets it is ahead of every embedding cost probe on average and never significantly behind any arm (the text router is 4.7 points cheaper on CodeContests, within noise). The embedding's success predictions are weaker on every dataset (AUC .73--.75 against .83 on LCB and Omni-MATH). We do not charge the embedding's own encoder pass, which would only favour our probes. On PCA-reduced 4B prefill features the three probes are within about 7 points of one another (one training set), so the gain comes from the representation rather than the regressor. \Cref{tab:size} varies the prefill model while keeping prompts, layers and both probes fixed (ten training sets). Every Qwen3 size from 0.6B to 4B saves 17.0--20.6\% on MMLU-Pro and 27.9--29.0\% on Omni-MATH against median pricing with its own success probes, and length $R^2$ rises with size (MMLU-Pro .33, .40, .44; Omni-MATH .61, .65, .66). The result is not specific to Qwen: prefills from three other families (Phi-4-mini~\citep{phi4mini}, Granite-3.3-2B, SmolLM2-1.7B~\citep{smollm2}) save 16.9--21.4\% and 25.1--28.2\%. None beats our prefill on average: ours saves 2.0--8.2 points relative to each of them, with pooled intervals excluding zero against Qwen3-0.6B, Granite and SmolLM2 on MMLU-Pro and including zero otherwise. Even the same-size Qwen3-4B hybrid release trails by 3.4 and 3.6 points on average (intervals including zero). Any small prefill works; size and release change the saving by a few points.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning.}
A router could instead train a small model end to end on the length labels, as CARROT does with RoBERTa~\citep{carrot}. We fully fine-tuned three such models on the same training problems and labels (DistilBERT 66M, jina-embeddings 137M and Qwen3-0.6B; learning rate and epoch chosen on calibration error) and used each as a cost estimator with our success predictions. On one training set, each saves less than our probe on Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro: ours saves 11.9--25.6\% relative to each, with intervals excluding zero. LCB is the exception for the largest model: there ours saves 11.7--14.9\% relative to the two smaller encoders, but averaged over ten training sets (each fine-tuned anew) only 6.5\% [$-$1.2, 12.2] relative to the fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B, ahead with an interval excluding zero in 7 of 10 training sets. Fine-tuning does not recover more than a linear probe of the frozen model: the same Qwen3-0.6B, frozen and read linearly, saves 31.9\% and 22.0\% relative to median pricing on Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro (one training set), more than its fine-tuned copy (20.0\% and 19.3\%), despite pairing with its own weaker success probes (length $R^2$ .62 against .53 on Omni-MATH, .33 against .38 on MMLU-Pro). The length information is already present in a pretrained decoder's prefill, and a linear probe recovers it from activations the router already computes, without training a model per router.

\paragraph{The prefill router's success pipeline.}
The prefill router~\citep{prefill} selects a layer, a pooling and a PCA dimension per target by cross-validation over the encoder's upper layers, concatenates the selected features and trains an ensemble of shared-trunk MLPs. We reimplemented this pipeline on the same encoder, splits and cost probe, and compared it with our per-route logistic probes on fixed layers. On one training set the two are tied on all three test sets: ours saves $-$0.1\% [$-$7.7, 4.0] on LCB, 1.1\% [$-$1.0, 3.0] on MMLU-Pro and $-$2.2\% [$-$5.7, 1.9] on Omni-MATH relative to it (success AUC .857 vs.\ .839, .715 vs.\ .714 and .829 vs.\ .842). On the two heterogeneous datasets the comparison splits: ours saves 8.4\% [$-$2.5, 18.4] relative to their pipeline on BIG-Bench Extra Hard, and their pipeline is ahead by 6.0\% [0.4, 11.2] on SuperGPQA, where success is barely predictable for either (AUC .58 and .57). Linear probes on a fixed set of layers are enough on four of five sets, and they make the cost probe a drop-in addition: the same features feed both. They are also cheaper to fit: on 16 CPU threads, our success and cost probes for five routes take about 3 minutes on LCB and MMLU-Pro (nine regularisation values per route, chosen on calibration), against about 7.4 minutes for the reimplemented pipeline (48 feature configurations $\times$ 5 folds per route, then ten networks); neither needs an encoder pass beyond the shared prefill, unlike the MixLLM-style and ZeroRouter estimators, which fit in seconds but read their own text encoder.

\section{Deployment}\label{app:deploy}
\paragraph{Routing near the top.}
The router does not beat the best single model at that model's accuracy. On every dataset deepseek-v4-flash is the most accurate route or within 1.6 points of it, at 17--37\% of the cost of gpt-oss-120b at high effort (\cref{tab:routes}). The router's most accurate policy, which sends each query to the route with the highest predicted success, ends 0.2--2.7 points below the most accurate route on seven datasets (it ties on AIME and is 2.0 points above on BIG-Bench Extra Hard), and where it reaches deepseek-v4-flash's accuracy it spends as much as calling it alone (AIME, SuperGPQA, BIG-Bench Extra Hard) or more (the coding datasets). Close to the top, routing buys little over calling deepseek-v4-flash alone: giving up one point of accuracy saves 5.1\% on AIME and 9.4\% on SuperGPQA (intervals excluding zero) and nothing significant elsewhere, while giving up five points saves 14.9--40.3\% on the six datasets other than APPS, BigCodeBench and CodeContests (LCB 32.6\%, Omni-MATH 33.1\%, MMLU-Pro 26.9\%, BIG-Bench Extra Hard 40.3\%), 1.5--1.8$\times$ what median pricing and 1.5--3.6$\times$ what a random mix of routes saves at the same accuracy. Below the top it is much cheaper than every other route at that route's accuracy: on the same six datasets, 45--83\% cheaper than gpt-oss-20b at medium effort and, where it reaches its accuracy, 50--92\% cheaper than gpt-oss-120b at high effort. The shortfall at the top is not specific to deepseek-v4-flash. In the pool of \cref{app:families} without it or gpt-oss-120b, the most accurate route, Nemotron-3-Super-120B, costs \$2.1--7.5 per thousand calls and the router again ends 1.6--3.2 points below it; there our router saves 44.7\% [36.6, 52.2] relative to the mix on LCB and 22.7\% [1.9, 38.3] on Omni-MATH, against 21.4\% and 10.9\% with median pricing, and on MMLU-Pro neither beats the mix (ours $-$7.6\% [$-$39.3, 20.5], median pricing $-$39.9\%).

\paragraph{Routing versus cascades.}
A cascade calls routes in sequence and stops when an answer is accepted~\citep{frugalgpt,automix}. To bound what any cascade could achieve on our datasets, we give it a perfect judge: every ordered plan of the five routes is called cheapest first, each attempt is judged by its true outcome at no cost, and the cascade stops at the first correct answer. A learned judge can only do worse. Even against this bound, one-shot routing with our probes (one training set) spends 29.7\% [19.6, 37.7] less at matched accuracy on LCB and 31.9\% [26.4, 37.3] less on Omni-MATH, because a cascade pays for every failed attempt before the one it accepts. On MMLU-Pro the two tie ($-$0.8\% [$-$6.1, 3.9]): the cheapest route solves 62\% of questions at about 6\% of the most expensive route's cost, so failed first attempts are cheap.

\paragraph{Deployable policies.}
Our main metric compares cost--accuracy frontiers on the test set; a deployed router must fix its trade-off $V$ in advance. For each estimator and each of ten accuracy targets spanning the band every estimator reaches on the calibration split, we choose the cheapest $V$ that reaches the target on calibration and apply it once to the test set. We compare its test cost with the median rule's deployed policies, interpolated at the accuracy it actually reaches (\cref{tab:deploy}; ten training sets). Our deployed policies save 19.6--37.9\% on LCB, Omni-MATH, MMLU-Pro, AIME, SuperGPQA and BIG-Bench Extra Hard; they cost 1.0--3.3\% more than our own test frontier at the accuracy they reach and miss their target by 2.2--5.3 points. They lead the best deployed external estimator on LCB, Omni-MATH and MMLU-Pro with intervals excluding zero in every training set, on AIME in 9 and SuperGPQA in 7 of 10, and tie it on BIG-Bench Extra Hard; they lead pricing from success on MMLU-Pro and BIG-Bench Extra Hard in every training set and on SuperGPQA in 7. Deployed savings exceed the frontier savings mainly because median pricing deploys less well: its calibration-chosen policies fall 8.6--16.5\% short of its own test frontier on MMLU-Pro, AIME, APPS, BigCodeBench, CodeContests and SuperGPQA, against at most 9.4\% for ours. On APPS, BigCodeBench and CodeContests our lead is within noise.


\paragraph{A frontier reference.}
To place the dataset against a frontier model, we called Claude Opus 5.5 (adaptive thinking at high effort, one sample per problem, same prompt and grader) on 100 random LiveCodeBench test problems. It solves 94.0\% at \$21.9 per thousand calls; it thinks on only 24\% of these problems and writes 954 output tokens on average. On the same problems deepseek-v4-flash alone reaches 88.8\% at \$0.8 per thousand calls, 94\% of the frontier model's accuracy at 4\% of its cost, and gpt-oss-120b at high effort 89.3\% at \$3.4; our router's most accurate policy reaches 87.7\% at \$1.6 (mean over the ten training sets), no better than deepseek-v4-flash alone, as on the full test set. The gap to the frontier model is a property of the pool's strongest open route, not of routing; the router's saving lies below that route's accuracy.

\paragraph{The router's own cost.}
The features come from one forward pass of Qwen3-4B over the prompt: 127--1,216 tokens on average per dataset, 19--28\,ms median latency on one H100 at batch size one. A router that predicts success from the prefill already pays for this pass, so the cost probe adds nothing to it. Against no routing at all, the pass is not free when the routed models are as cheap as ours (well under a dollar per thousand queries). Self-hosted at an assumed \$2 per H100-hour with unoptimised batched inference, it costs 2--10\% of our deployed saving on Omni-MATH, AIME, MMLU-Pro and SuperGPQA and about 30\% on BIG-Bench Extra Hard and APPS, whose prompts are long; at the input price of the smallest Qwen3 model listed on OpenRouter (14B, \$0.12 per million tokens), it costs 6--51\% of the saving, and more than the saving on APPS. Its share falls as the routed models get more expensive.

\paragraph{Per-query budgets.}
A per-query budget is the one setting where knowing when the strongest route will run long could beat calling it alone at any accuracy. We cap the cost of every call at $X$ (a call that would exceed it is cut off and fails), with $X$ at the 50th, 70th, 80th, 90th and 95th percentile of deepseek-v4-flash's cost per call on the training and calibration problems, and send each query to the route with the highest predicted probability of succeeding within the cap (ten training sets, full test sets). With our probe, that probability is the success probe times the chance that the route's length fits, from the predicted length and the training residuals. At the tightest cap the router beats calling deepseek-v4-flash alone on LCB (8.2 points of accuracy [5.8, 10.8]) and MMLU-Pro (7.0 [5.7, 8.3]), ahead in every training set, and the best single route by 5.3 and 0.9 points; on Omni-MATH it is 1.8 points ahead [$-$0.6, 4.3]. At looser caps it ties deepseek-v4-flash alone or falls 1--3 points behind (intervals excluding zero on MMLU-Pro and Omni-MATH at the 90th and 95th percentiles), at a spend between 19\% below and 11\% above deepseek-v4-flash's. Knowing each problem's realized length would beat deepseek-v4-flash alone by 9.3--15.5 points at the tightest cap and by 3.9--9.7 at the 70th percentile, so there is room under budgets; a probe trained directly on whether a call fits within the cap (a logistic probe per route and cap on the same prefill) reads it no better (within 2 points of ours everywhere, with the same AUC for whether deepseek-v4-flash fits, .81--.93). At the 95th percentile even known lengths do not beat deepseek-v4-flash alone, so there, too, the limit is the success probes. Against median pricing under the same caps our probe is ahead by up to 12.6 points (LCB at the 90th percentile, where the median rule sends queries to routes whose median length fits the cap but whose long outputs do not), and behind significantly at no cap.

\section{Software engineering}\label{app:agents}
We study software engineering in two regimes: one-shot patch generation, where per-query cost pays as on the coding datasets, and agents, where the price gaps between models dominate.

\paragraph{One-shot patches.}
On 1,432 SWE-Smith instances~\citep{swesmith}, each of the five routes writes one patch (deepseek-v4-flash pinned as elsewhere), labelled by executing the repository's tests in a sandbox. Pass rates are low and close together (25--37\%), but which instances are solvable is predictable from the issue (success AUC .80--.83 per route on the 389 test instances where every route returned a patch). Output length varies 5--28$\times$ between a route's 10th- and 90th-percentile instances and is only modestly predictable (log-length $R^2$ .09--.37). On one training set, per-query cost knowledge is worth 25.2\% [15.0, 31.1] over median pricing, and our probes save 14.5\% [5.8, 19.8], a capture of .58. They beat mean length by 7.8\% [3.5, 13.2], pricing from success by 9.2\% [2.2, 13.9], MixLLM-style embeddings by 6.8\% [2.5, 10.6] and prompt features by 18.2\% [8.1, 27.6]; difficulty bins (2.9\% [$-$0.3, 5.7]) and ZeroRouter (1.7\% [$-$4.7, 9.0]) are within noise. One-shot patch generation thus behaves like the coding datasets.

\paragraph{Agents: cost is real, varies by task and is stable.}
Public SWE-rebench trajectories~\citep{swerebench} give 111 tasks run five times by each of 13 models in one scaffold. Across models, agentic cost is dominated by the model: of the variance of log cost, 74\% is between models, 15\% between tasks, 6\% their interaction and 4\% run to run. Within a model, however, per-task cost spans 2.7--9.0$\times$ between the 10th and 90th percentile, and it is stable across runs (intraclass correlation .69--.92). Whether knowing it pays depends on the price gaps between the routed models, as in \cref{sec:when}: among all 13 models, whose prices per run span about 190$\times$, per-task cost knowledge is worth 8.5\% [$-$5.0, 17.8] (one-shot routing, success and cost from three runs, scored on the other two); among the seven open-weight models, whose prices span about 10$\times$, it is worth 25.7\% [6.3, 40.6], as much as on the reasoning datasets.

\paragraph{Agents: cost is only partly predictable before the run.}
From the issue text alone, the prefill's cost probe reaches out-of-fold $R^2$ of about zero on the 111 SWE-rebench tasks, too few to fit it. On 3,387 tasks with about 20 SWE-agent runs each (one model, Llama-3.1-70B~\citep{nebius}; cost proxied by cumulative context and output length), the per-task mean cost is reliable (split-half reliability .91) and the issue text predicts it at $R^2$ .29, still rising with data (.18 with 100 tasks, .32 with 2,710). This is lower than what the prefill reaches on most one-shot datasets (\cref{tab:bracket}). Reading the first ten steps of a cheap model's trajectory predicts other models' per-task cost at $R^2$ .16 and the same run's final cost at .28, which suggests exploring cheaply before pricing the expensive candidates.

\section{Pre-registered predictions}\label{app:prereg}
Before collecting two of the datasets, we recorded a forecast of whether the cost probe would pay there, from a cheap screen. The rule for Omni-MATH-500 was fixed before its screen: from one gpt-oss-20b-low draw per problem, if the prefill's cross-validated log-length $R^2$ was at least .50, predict a saving of at least 10\% over median pricing with an interval excluding zero; if at most .35, predict none; in between, make no call. The screen gave .67, and the forecast (a gain, and headroom of at least 15\%) was confirmed on the full set at the time, on one training set: 21.9\% [8.9, 33.5] at list prices with the pre-registered Thinking prefill, headroom 44.2\%. The same rule run as a screen elsewhere predicted a gain on APPS, which was not significant before deepseek-v4-flash was pinned and is at pinned billed prices (16.8\% [4.2, 30.4]) and wrong on AIME, where it predicted no gain and our probe saves 14.9\% [10.8, 20.1]: a single draw of the cheapest route understates how predictable lengths are. A second screen chose SuperGPQA and BIG-Bench Extra Hard as datasets where the dedicated probe should beat pricing from success: on one gpt-oss-20b-low draw per problem, length predicted directly from the prefill beat length predicted from a success probe by a margin as large as MMLU-Pro's ($R^2$ gap .61 and .40, against .66 on MMLU-Pro and .10 on Omni-MATH); it did on BIG-Bench Extra Hard (22.6 [12.3, 31.9]) and overstated the gap on SuperGPQA (15.3 [$-$0.6, 32.3]; ahead in 7 of 10 training sets).
