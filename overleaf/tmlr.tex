\begin{abstract}
Given a pool of language models and a cost budget, a policy must decide, per query, whether to
spend another draw, on which model, or to give up.  We show that a single quantity --- a
\emph{shared, model-independent difficulty estimate}, read from one cheap model's prefill before
any generation --- is enough to drive all three decisions, and we characterise exactly what it can
and cannot do.  Used for the \emph{whether}-to-attempt decision, it is the difference between a
working and a failing budgeted policy: replacing count-based beliefs with beliefs read from one
cheap prefill, changing nothing else, cuts cost at matched accuracy at \textbf{19 of 19 accuracy
targets across four pools and two task families}, including out-of-domain SWE-bench Verified.  The
value flows through abstention, and the mechanism is a knapsack: order problems by difficulty, and
the budget decides where the line goes.  Used for the \emph{which}-model decision, the same scalar
fails everywhere we test it --- on a deliberately complementary five-lab pool, every probe variant
captures none of the available routing gain, and on RouterBench the decomposition inverts: the
shared scalar is worse than not routing at all, while the model$\times$problem interaction carries
the entire gain.  Which half applies to a given pool is predictable in advance from measurable pool
structure.  The scalar transfers to models whose weights are never touched: the ordering is free,
and probabilities cost $\sim$25 labels on pools where the probe is strong (and do not transfer at
all where it is weak).  Methodologically, we introduce a shuffled-prediction control that separates
real information from a decision rule's mere response to per-problem dispersion --- a control no
routing paper we surveyed runs --- and a set of accounting corrections (matched budget grids,
randomised convex-hull frontiers, symmetric failure charges, fitting shared hyperparameters for
both arms) without which most of these comparisons are silently rigged in one direction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Deploying a pool of language models raises a sequential decision problem.  For each query an
operator may buy another draw from a model, switch models, or stop and return no answer, all under
a cost budget.  Prior work has formalised this as a resample-or-reroute problem
\citep{chen2026ror}, as threshold cascades solved by duality \citep{bouchard2026escalation}, and
as budgeted inference with a trained skip action \citep{zhao2026roi}; the underlying mathematics
--- Lagrangian relaxation of a constrained MDP, index policies, the value of inspection --- is
classical \citep{altman1999constrained,weitzman1979optimal,gittins1979bandit,
badanidiyuru2018knapsacks}.  What all of these systems lack is a per-query \emph{belief} about the
problem itself: every method we survey either conditions only on pool-level constants
\citep{chen2026ror} or requires fine-tuning the answering model itself \citep{zhao2026roi}.

This paper studies one specific belief source and what it is for.  A single forward pass of the
cheapest model in the pool --- a \emph{prefill}, with no generated tokens --- yields hidden states
from which simple linear heads predict each route's success probability and cost for the query at
hand.  Difficulty estimation from hidden states is established
\citep{llmalreadyknows2025,jeirt2025,irtnet2025,cmirt2026}; one model's activations predicting
another model's success is established \citep{prefillrouter2026,encodefailures2026}.  Our claims
are therefore not about the existence of the signal but about what it is \emph{for}: which
decisions it can support, under what accounting it demonstrably pays, and where it structurally
fails.

The characterisation has one positive and one negative half, and both are needed.

\paragraph{The positive half: whether.}
Placed inside a sequential cost-constrained policy, per-problem beliefs strictly beat the
count-based beliefs of the prior formulation.  Isolating the belief source --- the same policy,
the same formulation, constant costs on both sides --- reduces cost at matched accuracy at
\textbf{19 of 19 accuracy targets across four pools and two task families}
(Section~\ref{sec:headline}).  The mechanism is not routing: with the give-up action disabled the
same beliefs are worth nothing, and with it enabled they are worth up to $+43.7\%$
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).  Abstention at a binding budget is \emph{reallocation} --- skip
expensive problems to afford cheap ones --- so the policy is a knapsack over problems, not a
router over models.  In its cleanest form, one prefill and one ordering close 65--73\% of the
oracle gap in selective prediction on three benchmarks, with no MDP, no cost model and no budget
parameter (Section~\ref{sec:selective}).

\paragraph{The negative half: which.}
The same scalar cannot support model selection.  On a deliberately complementary pool --- five API
models from five labs, 52.6\% of problems contested, $+13.5$pt of available routing gain --- a
one-dimensional latent router uses one model 171 times out of 171, and every richer variant we
tried performs \emph{worse} (Section~\ref{sec:whethernotwhich}).  On RouterBench
\citep{hu2024routerbench} the decomposition inverts: the shared scalar is worse than not routing
at all ($-0.60\%$ AIQ) while the interaction term carries the entire gain ($+4.52\%$).  The
unifying observation is that a prompt representation carries \emph{shared factors} (this problem
is hard; this model is verbose) and not \emph{interaction terms} (this model rambles on this
problem); success decomposes the same way, which is why abstention works and routing does not
(Section~\ref{sec:unification}).

\paragraph{Transfer.}
Because the scalar is shared across the pool, it transfers to models whose weights are never
available: the \emph{ordering} is free (AUC is invariant to the affine transform a two-parameter
link can apply), and \emph{probabilities} cost about 25 labels per new model on pools where the
probe reaches AUC $\approx 0.79$ --- beating each new model's own base rate by $\sim$24\% of Brier
--- while on a pool where the probe is weak the base rate wins at every label budget
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer}).

\paragraph{Methodology.}
A persistent hazard in this area is that comparisons are rigged by accounting.  We introduce and
report four corrections that apply beyond this method: a \emph{shuffled-prediction control} that
separates information from mere dispersion (92--69\% of the belief head's gain survives it; only
31--49\% of the cost head's does; Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}); matched budget grids (an
under-sampled baseline grid manufactured most of an apparent formulation advantage;
Section~\ref{sec:robust}); the randomised convex-hull frontier, which is the achievable set of a
constrained MDP \citep{altman1999constrained}; and the rule that every conditioned quantity must
be selected on the objective the policy optimises, never on a predictor metric that is invariant
to what the policy consumes (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

Ordered by how well they replicate.  Several claims made during this project were retracted on
further measurement; the survivors are these.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{C0 (framing).}  The object is a shared difficulty scalar, and the paper is about
what it is for.  Its existence is not our claim \citep{jeirt2025,llmalreadyknows2025}.  Ours:
one cheap prefill reads it better than the deployable text baselines under matched pipelines
(Section~\ref{sec:battery}); it transfers cross-model including to weights never touched
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer}); and the decision-theoretic and validation machinery below holds for
any difficulty signal.
\item \textbf{C1.}  A per-problem difficulty prior, inside a sequential cost-constrained rule,
strictly beats count-based beliefs: 0 of 401 accuracy levels negative on LiveCodeBench (floor
$+2.90\%$) and 0 of 401 on TACO (floor $+0.93\%$) with costs held constant on both sides; in
budget units, $+26.5$pt and $+25.8$pt at a $0.25\times$ budget (Sections~\ref{sec:headline}
and~\ref{sec:reprform}).
\item \textbf{C1b.}  The belief and cost heads are \emph{substitutes, not complements}: each alone
buys $\sim$$+25$pt at a $0.25\times$ budget, stacking buys $-2\%$ to $-17\%$, and on one pool they
are literally one scalar (correlation $-0.52$ to $-0.71$; first principal component carries
71.4\%) while on another they are nearly orthogonal (PC1 43.7\%) --- which predicts in advance
where the cost head fails (Section~\ref{sec:headline}).
\item \textbf{C2.}  The value flows through abstention, and the mechanism is a knapsack.  With the
give-up action the beliefs are worth up to $+43.7\%$; without it, $\sim$0\%.  Stopping is exactly
$\max_m Q(s,m)\le 0$ --- complementary slackness, not an added mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \textbf{C2b (the most portable piece).}  The \emph{shuffled-prediction control}: permuting
predictions across problems while preserving marginals separates information from dispersion.  No
routing paper we surveyed runs this control, and without it ``conditioned beats constant'' is not
a claim about prediction quality (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item \textbf{C3.}  Cross-model transfer: the ordering is free, the probabilities cost
$\sim$25 labels --- or nothing at all, measurably, on pools where the probe is weak
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer}).
\item \textbf{C4.}  A scope law with two measured regimes.  On our pools (29--40\% of problems
unsolved by the entire pool) the prompt representation carries the shared factor and not the
interaction: six of seven applications needing the interaction fail for one identifiable cause.
On a 91.4\%-contested pool the decomposition inverts: shared scalar $-0.60\%$ AIQ, interaction
$+4.52\%$.  Same probe, opposite decomposition, and contested mass predicts which
(Sections~\ref{sec:whethernotwhich} and~\ref{sec:unification}).
\item \textbf{C5.}  Selective prediction is the cleanest form of the result: gap to oracle closed
64.9\% / 72.7\% / 43.8\% on three benchmarks; wasted spend at 50\% coverage cut 62\% / 54\% /
43\%.  No MDP, no cost model, no budget parameter (Section~\ref{sec:selective}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Prior art, stated compactly.}
The resample-vs-reroute framing as competing uses of a per-query budget, and the greedy
allocation policy we reproduce as our main baseline, are \citet{chen2026ror}
(\textbf{arXiv:2607.08665v1}; we cite the version explicitly because v3
\citep{chen2026rorv3} is a substantially different paper under a changed title that withdraws the
allocation framework and reports a negative identification result --- which is the open problem
our belief source addresses).  The shadow-price treatment of cascades is
\citet{bouchard2026escalation}.  The Lagrangian machinery, the randomised-mixtures theorem and the
convex-hull achievable set are \citet{altman1999constrained}; the reservation-value index is
\citet{weitzman1979optimal}; per-problem decoupling under a common price is the Whittle relaxation
\citep{whittle1988restless,gittins1979bandit}.  The \emph{goal} of budgeted inference with an
available skip action is \citet{zhao2026roi}; the rule $\max(0,\max_m(p_mR-c_m))$ itself is the
textbook Lagrangian dual of a constrained allocation, and we claim neither the goal nor the rule.
Encoder-target decoupling is \citet{prefillrouter2026}; we replicate it
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer}).  \emph{What survives as ours:} per-problem beliefs for an entire
heterogeneous pool from one prefill of a model that never generates; cross-model per-query cost
prediction; and the empirical finding that this is what makes the sequential rule work where
count-based beliefs do not.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Sequential and budgeted model selection.}
\citet{chen2026ror} formalise resample-vs-reroute as competing uses of a second call after a
fallible verifier accepts a candidate, allocate a per-query budget by an online greedy rule
driven by estimated marginal correctness per unit cost, and report a favourable cost--quality
Pareto front against single-route, one-commit-router, budget-aware best-of-$K$, cascade and
random-allocation baselines.  We reproduce that policy as our count-belief baseline.  Version 3
of the same arXiv ID \citep{chen2026rorv3} reconstructs the paper around a three-gate
identification framework and concludes that ``current evidence does not identify when to resample
rather than reroute'' --- a negative result that makes the per-problem belief question we answer
sharper, not moot: a policy cannot act on a distinction it cannot identify.  \citet{zhao2026roi}
budget a \emph{batch} of tasks under a global constraint with a trained solve-or-skip token, via
two fine-tuning stages of the answering model; \citet{bouchard2026escalation} derive Lagrangian
switching rules for threshold cascades.  \citet{seqroute2026} route within a shared multi-turn
budget without resampling or abstention; \citet{clusterescalate2026} escalate from clusters;
\citet{ucci2026} calibrate cascade thresholds from token margins; \citet{automix2024} mix models
behind a meta-verifier over self-verification; \citet{beliefguided2026} formalise
default-vs-escalate as a POMDP over response reliability with verifiable observations.  None of
these estimates a per-problem success and cost for every member of a pool from one non-generating
forward pass, and none combines resampling, rerouting and abstention under one price.
\citet{routingsurvey2026} map the routing/cascading distinction; \citet{routinggap2026} show part
of the celebrated router-to-oracle gap is single-draw label noise no router can capture, which is
why our tensors are multi-draw.

\paragraph{Activation-based prediction.}
\citet{prefillrouter2026} route from prefill hidden states and show encoder-target decoupling:
one model's activations can predict another model's success better than the target's own.  Their
router is single-commit, has no give-up action, prices cost by median training output tokens, and
trains jointly on full datasets with no label-efficiency analysis.  \citet{encodefailures2026}
probe a model's own pre-generation activations for its own success; \citet{scouting2026} feed a
searcher's hidden states to a router and honestly report that their no-router ablation ties.
\citet{quit2026knowing} formalise mid-generation abstention for a single model.
\citet{llmalreadyknows2025} read difficulty from the target model's own initial hidden state and
spend it on adaptive self-consistency; \citet{predictivescheduling2026} allocate a fixed token
budget across a batch from the target's own hidden states.  All of these are same-model;
ours is the cross-model direction.

\paragraph{Difficulty and Item Response Theory.}
Decomposing correctness into a shared item difficulty plus per-model ability is IRT, with an
active LLM line: \citet{jeirt2025}, \citet{irtnet2025} (routing from sentence embeddings),
\citet{cmirt2026} (unseen questions), and adaptive testing \citep{adaptivetesting2025}.  A Rasch
analysis of 67 models \citep{rasch2026} independently finds that most apparent routing headroom
is explained by a single latent difficulty dimension.  We claim no novelty for the latent; our
differentiators are the prefill source, the cost-constrained sequential setting with a give-up
action, and pool-extension label efficiency.

\paragraph{Cost prediction.}
\citet{howmuchleft2026} decode total response length from the prompt's last hidden state;
\citet{entropylength2026} predict output length for batching; \citet{egtp2026} and
\citet{trail2026} predict a model's own output length for scheduling.  All are own-model; the
cross-model direction --- one cheap prefill pricing \emph{other} models inside a
budget-constrained decision --- is the piece none of them do.
\citet{latencyrouting2026} and \citet{perquerycost2026} predict per-query cost for routing from
query embeddings toward latency objectives; \citet{semanticagreement2025} defer to a larger model
on semantic disagreement among ensemble generations, text-only.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
RouterBench \citep{hu2024routerbench} provides an 11-model pool with real pricing; its
non-decreasing convex hull of the individual models (the ``Zero Router'') is the correct
non-adaptive baseline and its own learned routers ``generally do not significantly outperform''
it --- we position against that hull, not against their routers.  \citet{llmrouterbench2026},
\citet{routingplateau2026}, \citet{coderouterbench2026}, \citet{universallatent2026} and
\citet{routingcollapse2026} cover related ground.

\paragraph{Agentic stopping.}
Adjacent in modality rather than mechanism: milestone-confidence early exit \citep{eet2026},
gradient-based run killing \citep{agentstop2026}, cheap trajectory monitors with restarts
\citep{failfast2026}, value-head escalation for software agents \citep{swerouter2026}, and
plan--execute cascades \citep{cope2026}.  These stop or escalate a \emph{single} model's run;
none prices a multi-model pool or abstains under a budget.

\paragraph{Classical foundations.}
Our formulation is textbook decision theory and we cite it as such: inspection-cost search and
reservation-value indices \citep{weitzman1979optimal,gittins1979bandit}, Lagrangian relaxation of
constrained MDPs with randomised mixtures of deterministic policies \citep{altman1999constrained},
restless-bandit decoupling under a common multiplier \citep{whittle1988restless}, bandits with
knapsacks \citep{badanidiyuru2018knapsacks}, adaptive submodularity for when greedy is
near-optimal \citep{golovin2011adaptive}, correlated Pandora variants for the cross-route coupling
we approximate away \citep{gergatsouli2023weitzman}, and sequential analysis for the stopping half
\citep{wald1945sequential}.

\section{Setting: budgeted sequential model selection}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{The per-problem MDP}
\label{sec:mdp}

A pool of $M$ routes (models) $\{1,\dots,M\}$, each able to produce up to $K$ i.i.d.\ draws for a
query $x$.  Draw $k$ of route $m$ yields an outcome $y_{m,k}\in\{0,1\}$ (verified correct or not)
at realised cost $c_{m,k}>0$ dollars.  Outcomes are fixed offline in a correctness tensor, so all
policies are compared by \emph{replay} on identical draws (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).

After some draws have been spent, the state is
\[
s \;=\; \bigl(x,\; \mathbf{n},\; \mathbf{r}\bigr),
\qquad \mathbf{n}=(n_1,\dots,n_M),\quad \mathbf{r}=(r_1,\dots,r_M),
\]
where $n_m$ counts \emph{failed} draws from route $m$ and $r_m$ the draws remaining.  The episode
ends on the first success, so every live state has all draws so far failed.  The action space is
$\mathcal{A}(s)=\{m : r_m>0\}\cup\{\bot\}$: buy one more draw on any route with capacity, or
abstain ($\bot$), ending the episode with no answer.  Every route is available at every step;
``resample'' and ``reroute'' are just names for the two cases of the same action
($n_m>0$ vs.\ $n_m=0$), as in \citet{chen2026ror}.

\subsection{The global budget and its dual}
\label{sec:dual}

The batch-level problem is
\[
\max \;\sum_i \E\bigl[\mathbb{1}\{\text{solved}_i\}\bigr]
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\sum_i \E[\text{cost}_i] \;\le\; B .
\]
With multiplier $\lambda\ge0$ the Lagrangian decouples across problems; writing $R = 1/\lambda$
--- \emph{the dollar value of a correct answer} --- each problem independently solves
$\max\;\E[\mathbb{1}\{\text{solved}\}]\,R - \E[\text{cost}]$, and sweeping $R$ traces the
cost--accuracy frontier.  This is the standard convexification: by
\citet{altman1999constrained}, the achievable set of a constrained MDP is the convex hull of its
deterministic policies, and the optimal constrained policy is a randomised mixture of them ---
which is exactly why we compare hulls, not grid points (Section~\ref{sec:robust}).  The same
construction makes randomised tests admissible in Neyman--Pearson and underlies index policies for
budget-coupled selection \citep{weitzman1979optimal,whittle1988restless,gittins1979bandit,
badanidiyuru2018knapsacks}; for LLM cascades the shadow-price framing is
\citet{bouchard2026escalation}.  The \emph{goal} of budgeted inference over many tasks with
skipping available is \citet{zhao2026roi}.

Within an episode, backward induction over the failure-count lattice gives
\[
V(\mathbf{n}) \;=\; \max\Bigl(\,\underbrace{0}_{\text{stop}},\;
\max_m\;\bigl[\,p_m(\mathbf{n})\,R \;-\; c_m \;+\; (1-p_m(\mathbf{n}))\,V(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{e}_m)\,\bigr]\Bigr),
\]
with $V\equiv0$ once every route is exhausted.  \textbf{The zero in the outer max is
abstention.}  It is not an added mechanism but the null action's value, and it fires exactly under
complementary slackness: the marginal dollar buys more accuracy elsewhere in the batch.  The
density rule of \citet{chen2026ror} ranks routes by $p_m/c_m$, a ratio of positive quantities
with no zero to cross, so it \emph{cannot} stop --- which is why we rank by \emph{surplus}
$p_mR-c_m$, which has a sign.

We deliberately do not claim the sequential machinery as a contribution; Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}
measures which components pay, and the answer is specific: the \emph{sequential} structure and the
stop action are load-bearing, the global price is marginal, and deep lookahead beyond $h{=}2$ does
not pay.

\subsection{Baselines, controls, and the naming problem}
\label{sec:arms}

Because three prior works use three different budget formulations, arm naming is load-bearing
\citep{chen2026ror,zhao2026roi}:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
baseline & budget & ranking statistic & abstains? \\
\midrule
\citet{chen2026ror} (v1) & \textbf{per-query} cap & density $p/c$ & no \\
\citet{zhao2026roi} & global batch (trained policy) & ROI (implicit) & yes (trained token) \\
ours & global, solved in the dual at price $R$ & surplus $pR-c$ & yes (zero-value action) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Our ``count beliefs'' arm caps spend per episode and ranks by density: a faithful reimplementation
of the published policy of \citet{chen2026ror}.  A global budget is strictly more powerful than a
per-query cap --- it can move money from a doomed problem to a solvable one --- so \emph{any}
margin against the capped baseline bundles a formulation advantage that is
\citet{zhao2026roi}'s (and the textbook's), not ours.  The arm that isolates what is actually ours
is \emph{count beliefs under our global dual} --- identical formulation, identical abstention
machinery, only the belief source differing.  Section~\ref{sec:reprform} reports the full $2\times
2$ and shows the formulation column is worth $+5.1\%$ to $+5.8\%$ at tight targets and negative
at loose ones on the matched grid: nearly all of every margin we report is representation.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The probe: one prefill, two linear heads}
\label{sec:probe}

All beliefs and costs come from one forward pass of the cheapest route.  Let $h(x)\in\R^d$ be the
mean-pooled last-layer-fraction hidden state of the \scout over the prompt tokens, computed
\emph{before any generation} --- one prefill, no decode:
\[
h(x)=\frac{1}{|T(x)|}\sum_{t\in T(x)} h^{(\ell)}_t(x).
\]
Mean pooling rather than last-token is a deliberate control: chat templates end differently
across model families, and reading position $N{-}1$ compares different objects across models
(Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).  Features are \emph{rich}: all layers $\times$ \{mean, last\},
concatenated ($d\approx 40{,}960$), which is free at inference --- the forward pass already
computes every hidden state --- and improves every head substantially over single-layer features.

Two linear heads per route, fit on the training split only, with $\ell$ and regularisation chosen
on the calibration split and never on test:
\[
\hat\theta_m(x)=\sigma\!\bigl(w_m^\top h(x)+b_m\bigr),
\qquad
\hat T_m(x)=\exp\!\bigl(\hat\beta_m^\top h(x)\bigr)\cdot\hat\varsigma_m ,
\]
where $\hat T_m$ predicts expected total tokens in log space with Duan's smearing estimator
\citep{duan1983smearing} restoring the conditional mean, and the head is \emph{fitted and shrunk
in dollar space} --- the space the policy acts in.  The cost estimate is
$\hat c_m(x)=\rho_m\,\hat T_m(x)$.

\paragraph{Cost of the method.}
Training: 551 problem-level labels, 0.16 CPU-seconds for all heads, ${\approx}40$\,KB of
parameters.  Inference: one 4B prefill (\$0.000149), already paid for under the mandatory-scout
protocol; the decision rule itself is arithmetic.  Crucially $h(x)$ comes from \emph{one} model,
so the pool's other members need never expose weights --- outcomes at training time and a price
at decision time suffice.

\subsection{Beliefs}
\label{sec:beliefs}

Let $\theta_m(x)\in(0,1)$ be the probability that a \emph{fresh} draw from route $m$ solves $x$.
After $n_m$ failures, a Beta--Bernoulli posterior with pseudo-count $\sigma$ gives the decayed
belief
\[
p_m(s)\;=\;\theta_m(x)\cdot\frac{\sigma}{\sigma+n_m}.
\]
Failures on route $m$ depress only route $m$: the routes are treated as conditionally independent
\emph{given $x$}.  This assumption is measured, not assumed.  Unconditionally the routes are
strongly coupled --- on clean LiveCodeBench, gpt-oss-120b's pass@1 is 97.7\% when the scout
succeeded on draw 0 and 69.9\% once the scout has failed once --- but that coupling \emph{is
problem difficulty}, which $\hat\theta_m(x)$ already reads off the prompt.  Conditioning on
$\hat\theta$, the scout-failure count adds essentially nothing to log-loss ($0.36886 \to
0.36875$).  \textbf{The probe performs the belief propagation that a coupled posterior would
otherwise have to do}, which is why the factorised form loses nothing here --- and why a
cross-route-coupled version of the baseline does not close the gap
(Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).

Three belief sources differ only in where $\theta$ comes from:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
family & $\theta_m$ & conditioned on the query? \\
\midrule
count beliefs (\citet{chen2026ror}-style) & $\bar\theta_m$, the train-set marginal solve rate & no \\
prefill beliefs & $\hat\theta_m(x)$ from the probe, decayed as above & yes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Count beliefs have no per-problem prior, so observed failures are their \emph{only} channel for
learning that a problem is hard --- and that channel is worth 28 points of pass@1
(Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).  At $\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{0}$ every problem has the identical belief
vector, which is why the count arm cannot abstain selectively at entry
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

\paragraph{The decay constant must be fitted for both arms.}
$\sigma=2.0$ was inherited from \citet{chen2026ror} unfitted --- by them and, until we fitted it,
by us.  Maximum-likelihood constants on calibration are $0.5$--$1.35$ for our beliefs and
$0.25$--$1.09$ for count beliefs; $\sigma{=}2$ loses all six route$\times$dataset held-out
log-loss cells.  Crucially, giving \emph{both} methods their own fitted constant costs us more
than it gains: on TACO our utility-level win goes from 57/96 swept prices to 46/96
(Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).  \textbf{An unfitted hyperparameter in the baseline is a silent
advantage for whoever tunes theirs}; any paper reusing a prior method's pseudo-count should fit
it for both arms before claiming a win.

\subsection{Decision rule and abstention}
\label{sec:rule}

At a live state, the value of spending one draw on route $m$ is $Q(s,m)=p_m(s)\cdot R - c_m(x)$,
and abstaining is worth exactly zero:
\[
\pi(s)=\begin{cases}
\bot & \text{if } \max_{m} Q(s,m)\;\le\;0,\\[4pt]
\arg\max_{m} Q(s,m) & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
Abstention needs no threshold and no extra head: it is the zero-value action.
$Q$ is \emph{not} scale-invariant, and that matters: the density rule ranks routes identically at
every valuation and always prefers the cheapest, whereas the dollar difference lets an expensive
route win when the problem looks solvable and lets \emph{every} route lose when it does not.

\subsection{The mechanism: a knapsack over problems}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\paragraph{The shuffled-prediction control.}
With constant per-route belief \emph{and} constant per-route cost, the utility $p_mR-c_m$ is
identical for every problem: the baseline can only attempt-everything or attempt-nothing (measured:
0.0\% abstention).  \emph{Any} per-problem dispersion unlocks partial abstention, informative or
not --- so ``conditioned beats constant'' conflates two things.  The control permutes the
predicted vectors across problem ids (marginals preserved exactly, pairing destroyed) and re-runs
the full replay:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llccccc}
\toprule

\midrule
LCB & beliefs & 26.8\% & 28.8\% & 52.7\% & +2.0pt & \textbf{92\%} \\
LCB & cost & 26.8\% & 40.4\% & 53.3\% & +13.6pt & 49\% \\
TACO & beliefs & 19.6\% & 27.1\% & 43.7\% & +7.5pt & \textbf{69\%} \\
TACO & cost & 19.6\% & 35.5\% & 42.6\% & +15.9pt & 31\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The belief head survives the null on both benchmarks --- 92\% and 69\% of its gain is destroyed by
shuffling, so it carries real per-problem information.  The cost head does not, and TACO is where
it fails hardest (31\%), resolving an apparent contradiction: TACO's cost head predicts
\emph{success} at chance yet still appeared to buy $+23$pt at a tight budget.  It was mostly
buying dispersion.  \textbf{Report the shuffled arm beside every conditioned arm}; it is the only
comparison that separates ``we predicted something useful'' from ``we gave a degenerate decision
rule something to vary on'', and no routing paper we surveyed runs it.

\paragraph{The null ladder: random allocation beats the count-based greedy rule.}
Choosing routes uniformly at random --- \emph{no information at all} --- is cheaper than the
count-based density rule at every accuracy target ($+3.5\%$ to $+6.6\%$), because the density
$p_m/c_m$ is scale-invariant in cost and systematically over-buys the cheapest route, while random
selection diversifies.  Ours beats random allocation by $+46.7\%$ at the 50\% target and is
positive at all five.  ``We beat \citet{chen2026ror}'' is the weaker claim; \textbf{``we beat
random'' is the load-bearing one}: pool-level beliefs plugged into a density rule are not merely
less useful than per-problem ones --- they are worse than nothing.  (Single seed, one pool;
replicated before quotation.)

\subsection{Cross-model transfer: the ordering is free, the probabilities cost $\sim$25 labels}
\label{sec:transfer}

\paragraph{The claim.}
AUC is invariant to a positive affine transform, so a two-parameter response curve cannot change a
ranking: the \emph{ordering} of a new model's success transfers with \emph{no labels}.  Labels buy
calibration.  Fitting the difficulty latent from only the three local routes, then applying it to
five models it has never seen (one per lab, API-only, weights never touched), with an
$N$-label two-parameter link scored by Brier on 171 test problems against each peer's own base
rate:

The latent beats the new model's base rate for every peer at every $N$, by ${\sim}24\%$ of Brier
at $N{=}25$, and saturates at $N\approx25$ ($0.1946 \to 0.1857 \to 0.1801$ for $25\to50\to170$).
The result survives the label-quality problem (one peer has 25.7\% empty and 38.6\% truncated
outputs; dropping it moves the mean by 0.001).  Within the local pool, 25 labels through the
shared latent beat 550 labels of a dedicated high-dimensional probe (test AUC 0.7898 vs.\
0.7755), because there are only two parameters to fit.

\paragraph{The boundary condition, measured.}
The transfer does \emph{not} work everywhere.  On SWE-bench Verified the new model's base rate
wins at every $N$ up to all 219 available: the latent ranks better than a dedicated probe (AUC
0.616--0.670 vs.\ 0.517--0.629) but is \emph{worse calibrated than simply knowing how often the
new model succeeds}.  The discriminating quantity is the latent's own strength: peer AUC
0.746--0.812 on LiveCodeBench against 0.616--0.670 on SWE-V.  \textbf{Pool extension works where
the probe is strong and fails where it is weak}, which is a boundary condition, not a
contradiction, and it is measurable before committing to a pool.

\paragraph{The smallest model is also the best encoder.}
Scaling the probe (identical pipeline, identical split, only the prefill model changes): the 4B
scout is the best encoder on average (mean test AUC 0.8677) and 42$\times$ cheaper to prefill than
the dearest alternative (gpt-oss-120b, 0.8620); a \textbf{0.69B} model retains 95\% of pool-solvability
AUC (0.818 vs.\ 0.865) while solving almost nothing itself.  A model far too weak to solve these
problems still separates solvable from hopeless --- the probe reads a property of the
\emph{problem}, not of the solver.  This replicates \emph{encoder-target decoupling}
\citep{prefillrouter2026} on a sequential pool and strengthens it on cost grounds: the usual
objection ``a bigger probe would predict better'' is false here and measurably so.  Consistent
with \citet{prefillrouter2026}, larger open-weight encoders do not improve the signal here either;
we read this as the signal being problem-level difficulty rather than a property of
representational geometry, and state it as a competing hypothesis with an ablation rather than a
refutation.

\subsection{Whether, not which: the shared scalar does not route}
\label{sec:whethernotwhich}

\paragraph{A complementary pool, and none of the gain is captured.}
The five-lab peer pool has abundant routing headroom: best single model 71.9\%, union 85.4\%,
52.6\% of problems contested.  Our cascade pool offers $+3.6$pt between best and union; the peer
pool offers $+13.5$pt.  Every probe variant we tried captures none of it:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
router & accuracy & cost & acc/\$ & models used \\
\midrule
1-D shared latent (2 params/model) & 70.2\% & \$0.0169 & \textbf{41.4} & \textbf{1 of 5} \\
latent + 8 shared PCA dims & 70.8\% & \$0.0226 & 31.4 & 5 of 5 \\
per-model probe on activations & 64.9\% & \$0.0284 & 22.9 & 5 of 5 \\
always best single model & 71.9\% & \$0.0321 & 22.4 & --- \\
\textbf{oracle cheapest-that-solves} & \textbf{85.4\%} & \$0.0179 & \textbf{47.7} & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The collapse is structural, not a fitting failure.  All five response curves are monotone in the
\emph{same} scalar with similar slopes, so the ordering is nearly constant across problems: one
model dominates the whole latent range and $\arg\max$ picks it 171/171 times.  A one-dimensional
latent can express \emph{how hard is this problem} --- which is a threshold, i.e.\ abstention ---
but not \emph{which model suits this problem}, which requires the ordering to change.  Adding
dimensions makes it worse (31.4 and 22.9 vs.\ 41.4): extra variance, no signal.  \textbf{The
failure is not dimensionality; the information is not in the prompt representation.}

\paragraph{RouterBench: the decomposition inverts.}
On RouterBench \citep{hu2024routerbench} (11 models, 3.8\% pool-unsolvable, 91.4\% contested),
with the scout's activations extracted for every prompt and their Zero Router and AIQ metric:
weighted AIQ is $+3.91\%$ for activations against $+2.43\%$ for TF-IDF and $-6.03\%$ for the
shuffled control.  Decomposed: information $+9.94\%$; \textbf{interaction $+4.52\%$};
\textbf{abstention $+0.10\%$} --- and \textbf{the shared scalar alone is $-0.60\%$, worse than not
routing at all}.  On a 91.4\%-contested pool, all of the gain is the model$\times$problem
interaction; on our pools (29--40\% unsolvable) the interaction is unlearnable and the shared
scalar carries everything.  \emph{Same probe, opposite decomposition, and contested mass predicts
which.}  Two honesty notes: $+3.91\%$ is a modest win in a regime where RouterBench's own routers
``generally do not significantly outperform'' the Zero Router --- we do not claim to beat their
routers --- and an oracle-dataset router (told which benchmark each prompt came from) is worth
$-0.07\%$, so none of the gain is benchmark identification.

\subsection{The unification: shared factors are predictable, interaction terms are not}
\label{sec:unification}

Decomposing both success and cost on the peer pool gives one mechanism for every positive and
negative result in the paper:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
component & what it is & predictable from a prefill? \\
\midrule
rank-1 problem factor & ``this problem is long / hard'' & \textbf{yes} --- the shared latent \\
rank-1 model factor & ``this model is verbose, that one terse'' & \textbf{yes} --- a constant \\
\textbf{the 27.9\% interaction} & ``\emph{this} model rambles on \emph{this} problem'' & \textbf{no} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Per-peer cost is partly predictable from the scout's activations (dollar $R^2$ of 0.390, 0.246,
0.158, 0.144, 0.032 across the five peers), but \textbf{the cost \emph{ratio} between peers is
not: 0 of 10 pairs positive} --- and the ratio is exactly what route selection compares.  The
structure genuinely exists: the peer log-cost matrix is not rank-1 (first singular component
explains 72.1\%), model verbosity constants span 14$\times$, and the cheaper model genuinely
changes from problem to problem.  There is something to route on; we cannot see it.

\emph{Success} = shared difficulty (predictable, so \textbf{abstention works}) + interaction
(invisible, so \textbf{routing fails}).  \emph{Cost} = shared length (predictable, so the cost
\textbf{level} works) + verbosity constant + interaction (invisible, so the cost \textbf{ratio}
fails).  One mechanism explains six symptoms: per-candidate probing buys nothing once its probes
are priced (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}); extra PCA dimensions make routing worse;
cost ratios are negatively predictable; the one-dimensional router collapses to one model; TACO's
cost head fails where the shared component is weakest; and beliefs pay only through the give-up
action.  The claim is falsifiable: find a representation that predicts the interaction term, and
routing becomes possible.

\subsection{Selective prediction: the cleanest form}
\label{sec:selective}

Score every problem by predicted pool-solvability from the one scout prefill, attempt the top
$c$ fraction, and measure \emph{risk} --- the share of attempted problems that nothing in the pool
solves (pure wasted spend).  No MDP, no cost model, no budget parameter:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
\toprule
benchmark & $n$ & unsolvable & AUC & AURC & oracle risk & \textbf{gap closed} \\
\midrule
LiveCodeBench & 171 & 15.2\% & 0.759 & 0.062 & 0.013 & \textbf{64.9\%} \\
TACO & 168 & 41.1\% & 0.838 & 0.185 & 0.100 & \textbf{72.7\%} \\
SWE-bench Verified & 369 & 28.7\% & 0.703 & 0.182 & 0.046 & \textbf{43.8\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Wasted spend at 50\% coverage: 5.8\% vs.\ 15.2\% ($-62\%$), 19.0\% vs.\ 41.1\% ($-54\%$), and
16.3\% vs.\ 28.7\% ($-43\%$), respectively.  One cheap prefill, one ranking, and the money spent
on problems nothing can solve is roughly halved --- including out of domain, where the probe is
weakest (SWE-bench's 43.8\% is consistent with its lower AUC, and is a floor: the recorded probe
is stronger than the reconstruction used here).

\subsection{Robustness and honest accounting}
\label{sec:robust}

\paragraph{Matched budget grids.}
The budget-swept (baseline) arm's grid was 17 linear points; ours, 96 geometric.  Below \$0.02
that is 3 points vs.\ 55, exactly where tight-budget claims were read.  Under the matched grid the
baseline gains most at the tight end (12.3\% cheaper at the 50\% target, 8.7\% at 60\%) and our
margin falls from $+54.9$ to $+48.6$ and $+27.5$ to $+20.6$ --- and, critically, the
\emph{formulation} attribution falls from $+16.7$/$+13.9\%$ to $+5.1$/$+5.8\%$, turning negative
at loose targets (Section~\ref{sec:reprform}).  A sparse grid is not conservative: greedy
allocation under a cap is not concave in budget, so a denser grid can surface points above the
chord.  \textbf{Sweep both arms under the same spacing law.}

\paragraph{The frontier must be convexified.}
``Cheapest arm reaching target $T$'' evaluates a family at a single swept operating point, and the
comparison silently switches arm when no point lands near $T$ --- seed sd fell 2--4$\times$ at
exactly the unstable cells once mixtures were allowed on both sides, at the cost of cutting the
flattering numbers too ($+56.0 \to +42.2\%$ at 50\%).  Randomised mixtures are achievable by
construction \citep{altman1999constrained}, so the hull --- not the grid --- is the frontier.  All
frontier numbers in this paper are hulls, and utility at the matched price $R$ is reported
alongside as the objective-level check (84 of 96 swept prices won, unanimous across 8 seeds, with
the 12 losses being the 12 lowest prices --- the probe fee and nothing else, the honest floor of
the method).

\paragraph{Charging for abstention.}
Abstention is free in our accounting, so part of the tight-budget advantage might be nothing but
declining work.  Pricing every abstention at a full gpt-oss-120b call (\$0.02638): against the
\emph{capped} baseline the tight-budget win collapses ($-38.5\%$ at 50\%) --- but that collapsed
part is the value of \emph{having} a give-up action at all, which is \citet{zhao2026roi}'s and the
textbook's contribution, not ours.  Against the honest baseline (count beliefs under the same
dual, which also abstains and is also charged) the advantage is robust to any price:
$+23.9$/$+10.6$/$+12.9$/$+6.0\%$ at one full-call price, $+16.9$ to $+6.4\%$ at double, and it
\emph{grows} at the 80\% target as the price rises, because our abstentions are better targeted.
\textbf{Against a baseline that abstains on the same budget with count-based beliefs, activation
beliefs cut cost at matched accuracy even when every abstention is charged the price of the
dearest route.}

\paragraph{A weak verifier: the claim survives but roughly halves.}
All results above assume the verifier's acceptances are correct.  Under a verifier with an
11.01\% false-accept rate (11$\times$ the 1.0\% of \citet{chen2026ror}'s agreement verifier ---
we are testing a more adversarial regime, not the same one), scored against the truth so a false
accept ends the episode with a wrong answer shipped: the belief claim survives at every reachable
target but halves ($+40.8 \to +19.6\%$ at 50\%, $+19.2 \to +10.5\%$ at 60\%), reproducing
\citet{chen2026ror}'s own verifier-gating ablation --- corroboration of the prior work, not a
weakness of ours.  The bigger effect is the ceiling: reachable accuracy collapses from 84.8\% to
62.1\%, because episodes terminate on a lie.  \textbf{Every high-target number in this paper is
conditional on a near-oracle verifier}, and we report both regimes side by side.  Letting the
policy \emph{distrust} a positive verification signal --- banking a candidate worth $R\,q_m$ and
continuing --- recovers 6.3 points of that ceiling; the framing is taken (a POMDP with a noisy
verifier; \citet{beliefguided2026}), the combination (multi-route resampling, rerouting and
abstention with per-route acceptance reliability) is not, and per-problem $q_m$ is predictable
(ICC 0.964/0.856/0.882) because public-test verifier failure is systematic rather than random.

\paragraph{Symmetric failure accounting.}
In verifier-rich domains a delivered wrong answer and an announced decline are the same object up
to one test execution, so an honest accounting charges \emph{every} failure equally, not only our
abstentions.  At matched accuracy the failure rate is equal by construction, so this adds a
near-identical constant to both arms --- and the advantage survives any charge: $+26.0$/$+14.1$/
$+14.2$/$+5.0$/$+7.3\%$ at a 1:1 price and still $+3.4$ to $+6.0\%$ at \emph{ten times} the
dearest route's price per failure.  The advantage cannot be priced away; it merely shrinks toward
the ratio 1 as any constant charge must.  An earlier asymmetric accounting (pricing our give-up
while letting the baseline's failures go free) produced an alarming $-38.5\%$ and was wrong.

\paragraph{The cap does not bound realised spend.}
A per-query cap implemented against \emph{estimated} costs bounds planned spend only: a third of
the capped baseline's episodes exceed their cap (up to 22.3$\times$), because realised costs drift
from estimates and nothing corrects the drift.  A genuinely binding cap requires predicting the
\emph{next} draw's cost, which a per-query cost head enables and constant per-route costs cannot.
Relatedly, the three-state decomposition (solved / abstained / covered-and-wrong) shows the two
policy families fail differently: our value arms' failures are almost all \emph{declines}, the
capped baseline's are almost all \emph{shipped wrong answers}.  Wasted spend is the norm for
every arm (63.5--95.9\% of all spend, rising as the budget tightens) --- at matched accuracy we
waste 16--18\% fewer dollars, and what our waste buys (a flagged decline rather than a silently
wrong answer) is a deployment argument, not a cost one.

\paragraph{Price ratio: the method needs a dearest-to-cheapest gap.}
The spread the method exploits is basis-dependent, and the threshold is higher than we first
reported: at a 3$\times$ price ratio the method is catastrophic ($-118.9\%$ at the 65\% target),
at 6$\times$ still negative at three of four targets, at 10$\times$ break-even, and a solid win
needs ${\sim}25\times$, at which point the result is stable through list price (40$\times$).  Our
pool contains two mixture-of-experts models, for which the ratio swings from 1.3$\times$ (active
parameters, flat GPU price) to 40$\times$ (total parameters, our node estimate) on accounting
alone --- so \textbf{we state the threshold in the units the operator pays and report the full
sweep}, rather than asserting transfer.  Parameter count, the standard proxy in this literature
\citep{chen2026ror}, overstates the headroom on MoE pools.

\subsection{Diagnostics: where the remaining headroom lives}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

\paragraph{Oracle bounds.}
Replacing exactly one component with ground truth, at the 80/84\% accuracy targets where nothing
we tried moves anything: perfect \textbf{stopping} is worth $+44.0$/$+65.0\%$, perfect
\textbf{routing} only $+8.3\%$ at 84\%, perfect \textbf{beliefs} $+65.5$/$+73.4\%$ --- of which we
capture 4\% and 9\%.  Tight-target oracle cells are unreadable (the oracle arms attach to a
frozen-price policy whose hull cannot span the tight end) and are reported as n/a rather than as
numbers.  \textbf{The loose end is a belief-quality problem}: at high coverage the binding
constraint is knowing which problems are hopeless, not choosing among routes, and no structural
variant can manufacture that.  This overturns an earlier hypothesis of ours that the stopping
channel was nearly closed: stopping is untouched exactly where it matters most.

\paragraph{There is essentially no irreducible label noise in success.}
Between-problem ICC of observed per-problem solve rates, binomial variance removed, is
0.975/0.917/0.924 across the three routes: 92--98\% of the spread is real between-problem signal,
so the oracle-belief headroom is genuinely reachable, not a mirage of draw noise.  (Cost is
different: its ICC is 0.841, so its ceiling is materially below $R^2{=}1$.)

\paragraph{Lookahead does not pay; the transition model caps it.}
The exact solve ($h{=}18$, 342 lattice nodes --- the truncation was inherited and never
revisited; 85$\times$ the $h{=}2$ cost and still negligible) does not beat $h{=}2$: $h{=}4$, 6
and 18 are essentially identical at the tight end and $h{=}18$ is \emph{worse} at 84\% (3.9 $\to$
2.9\%).  Diagnosis: at $h\ge2$ the lattice reuses the root belief at every node, asserting that
failing route $m$ says nothing about route $m'$ --- which is false (and is exactly the correlated
Pandora problem \citep{gergatsouli2023weitzman}).  Independence over-values continuation while
truncation under-values it; deeper search removes the truncation error while compounding the
transition error.  \textbf{More exact optimisation over a wrong model buys nothing} --- the same
shape as the belief finding.

\paragraph{A learned per-problem decay works, and our probe beats it.}
A factorised scorer that emits its own per-problem decay learns something true (ground-truth
hazard ratios split cleanly by difficulty) and beats count beliefs at four of five targets ---
but our probe beats \emph{it} at four of five (beliefs-only, one replay so nothing else differs:
$+32.1$/$+15.7$/$+7.6$/$+2.6$/$-0.8\%$).  A learned decay is a one-parameter summary of what the
prefill already carries in full.

\paragraph{Select every component on the objective the policy optimises.}
Four interventions each improved their own predictor metric and each made the frontier worse
(belief penalty on AUC: $+0.024$ AUC, $-4.3$pt floor; cost penalty on $R^2$; cross-route coupling
$+0.024$ AUC, $-23.8$pt; isotonic recalibration: perfect by construction, $-0.4$ to $-1.9$pt).
The cause is structural: AUC is invariant to monotone transforms, the rule
$\arg\max_m(p_mR-c_m)$ consumes \emph{values}, and abstention fires on $\max_m Q \le 0$ ---
better-regularised predictors are more \emph{compressed}, and the rule needs \emph{spread}.
Selecting on the calibration frontier instead trades regimes rather than dominating ($+3.88$pt at
$0.25\times$ on 5/5 seeds, $-2.60$pt at $1.0\times$).  \textbf{A head with strictly better ranking
can make the policy worse.}

\paragraph{The strongest honest baseline variant does not close the gap.}
Giving count beliefs a cross-route difficulty channel (a second decay on other routes' failures,
swept over its coupling constant) makes our margin \emph{larger}, not smaller
($+51.7$/$+21.2$/$+12.6$/$+7.1\%$ vs.\ $+45.8$/$+12.9$/$+12.1$/$+5.5\%$ against plain counts):
letting the baseline infer difficulty from observed failures makes it spend more, not less ---
consistent with the entry-decision analysis, since observed failures are the only channel count
beliefs have, and they are expensive.

\paragraph{Per-candidate probing loses once priced.}
Probing every model in the pool --- one prefill each, including the 120B --- costs 48.5$\times$
our single scout probe.  Given away free, per-candidate probing wins at five of six targets
($+0.6$ to $+8.6$pt), so the better predictor does convert; charged what it costs, it is worse at
\emph{all six}, by 10.9 to 38.5pt.  The extra signal is worth less than one prefill on the 120B.
The deployment argument (one model's weights, not the pool's) rests on price, not on a failure to
convert --- and this is the same conclusion the peer-pool routing analysis reaches from the other
direction.

\paragraph{Method variants, and the pattern that matters.}
Six structural variants were run against their own controls: a two-constraint policy (per-episode
cap \emph{and} global price swept jointly; $+4.1$/$+7.1$/$+11.4$/$+2.4\%$, the best new result ---
neither the cap nor the pure Lagrangian), an exact posterior over success counts ($+11.0$/$+5.7$/
$+5.9\%$ tight/mid), a free-start protocol ($+9.2$/$+2.2\%$ for us and \emph{exactly $+0.0\%$ at
every target for the count baseline}, because its beliefs are identical at entry --- a mechanism
confirmation), a winner's-curse shrink ($+12.1\%$ tight only), a cross-route coupling correction
(mixed), and a quantile cost head (directional; only the conservative tail positive).  Four of
five improving variants peak at the 50\% target around $+8$ to $+12\%$ and fade by 80\%: they are
all the \emph{same} intervention --- improving the give-up decision --- and will stack
sub-additively.  \textbf{Stop tuning the give-up rule at the tight end; the loose end is a belief
problem} (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Pool composition.}  Two primary benchmarks, both competitive programming, plus
SWE-bench Verified as an out-of-domain check (single split, $n{=}369$) and RouterBench for the
opposite regime.  The interaction-is-unlearnable claim rests on six failed applications across
our pools and one inverted decomposition on RouterBench; more regimes would sharpen it.
\item \textbf{The cost head is a contribution with a documented failure mode.}  It is a strict
improvement on LiveCodeBench and actively harmful on TACO above the 40\% target; the choice is a
per-pool decision made on calibration, never post hoc on test.  Cost prediction fails on runaway
generation, which is prompt-unpredictable by measurement.
\item \textbf{Price-ratio dependency.}  A solid win needs a ${\sim}25\times$ dearest-to-cheapest
ratio; MoE accounting can move a pool across that threshold on basis alone
(Section~\ref{sec:robust}).
\item \textbf{Verifier conditionality.}  High-target results assume a near-oracle verifier; under
an 11\%-false-accept verifier the ceiling collapses from 84.8\% to 62.1\% and the gain halves
(Section~\ref{sec:robust}).
\item \textbf{Seed and split coverage.}  The null-ladder result, the method variants, the
RouterBench decomposition and the SWE-bench rows rest on single seeds or single splits; the
headline table is 5-seed/3-seed.  Every number should be reproduced at budgets, seeds and pools
where it was not measured before being relied on.
\item \textbf{Serving configuration.}  Outcomes were collected under our own serving stack;
another provider's defaults could shift absolute solve rates.  All comparisons are
within-collection.
\item \textbf{The readout correction} (mean- vs.\ last-token pooling reverses cross-model
orderings because chat templates end differently) is measured on 3 models on one benchmark.
\item \textbf{Lower ceiling than the baseline at maximum price.}  With the value of a correct
answer driven arbitrarily high, both policies buy everything and converge; our abstention forgoes
the last tenths of a percent of the pool ceiling, and utility at matched price is reported
alongside the frontier for exactly this reason.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A single shared difficulty scalar, read from one cheap model's prefill before anything runs, is
enough to drive a budgeted sequential policy --- and what it is \emph{for} is now measured rather
than assumed.  It supports the \emph{whether}-to-attempt decision, where it turns a
count-belief policy into one that cuts cost at matched accuracy at every target on every pool we
have (19/19), because abstention at a binding budget is a knapsack over problems and the prefill
supplies the per-problem information the knapsack needs.  It does not support the
\emph{which}-model decision, on pools where routing headroom is abundant, for a reason that is
measurable in advance: a prompt representation carries shared factors and not interaction terms.
It transfers to models whose weights are never touched, free as an ordering and at ${\sim}25$
labels as a probability --- or not at all, measurably, where the probe is weak.  The remaining
headroom is belief quality at high coverage, where we recover single-digit percentages of what
perfect beliefs are worth, and the open problem is one we can now state precisely: find more
per-problem information in the prompt, or buy a little evidence.  Everything the formulation
needs beyond that is classical \citep{altman1999constrained,weitzman1979optimal}, and the
formulation column was never ours; the representation was, and the controls needed to show it
--- shuffled predictions, matched grids, hull frontiers, symmetric charges, jointly fitted
hyperparameters --- are, we think, the most portable thing in this paper.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
All results are replays over stored correctness tensors; every headline number traces to a
committed script and a named run.  We release the replay harness, the pool-construction code, and
the probe training pipeline.
